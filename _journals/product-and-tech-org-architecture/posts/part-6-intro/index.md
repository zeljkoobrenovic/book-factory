> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **Planning can take most of a quarter and still not align the work.** In 2026 Corvane's quarterly planning took nine of thirteen weeks, and three teams ended up building the same integration.
> * **Growth separates information from the right to decide.** Goodwill and communication do not put them back together, because they cover only the paths between people who already talk.
> * **Three mechanisms produce alignment.** Decision rights that are recorded, planning in separate steps joined by team objectives, and a small set of forums and written documents.

<br>

In 2026, at about 500 people, Corvane's quarterly planning took nine of the quarter's thirteen weeks. Corvane is the fictional company this book follows. Its thirty or so teams sat in four product groups, each under a director, and the planning was a series of meetings with every director in which budget, headcount, the roadmap and team goals were negotiated together. At the end of the nine weeks, **three teams were building the same integration**.

You have probably sat through planning like this, which consumes much of the time it plans. The usual answers are to communicate more, with one more meeting to align people, or to cut the process and trust people to coordinate. Both skip a question. **Which decisions is planning made of?** Nine weeks of meetings is already a great deal of communication, so more of it was not what Corvane lacked. And with less process the three teams would still not have known of each other. With everything negotiated together, nobody at Corvane could say which decisions the nine weeks were for, who made each one, and where it was written down.

Part VI starts from that question and widens it from planning to **alignment**, by which this book means that the work of many teams adds up. It argues that **alignment is produced by three explicit mechanisms**: decision rights that put each decision next to its information and record it; a planning process in which allocating capacity and choosing outcomes are separate steps, joined by team objectives; and a small set of recurring forums and written documents that carry information along designed paths. Each of these terms is defined below. This page first says why alignment does not arise by itself as an organization grows, and then gives the three mechanisms. Three chapters then answer three questions in order: who decides what and how the decision is recorded, how capacity and outcomes are planned in one process, and which forums and documents an organization needs at each size.

## Why Asking for Alignment Does Not Produce It

Why does alignment have to be produced? Because growth separates two things that a small company holds together. In a company of twenty, the person who knows what a customer needs and what the code can do is usually the person who decides. As the organization grows, **the information a decision needs and the right to make it are separated by default**: the information stays with the teams, each of which sees only its own part, and the right to decide moves up or is never stated. Three things are then missing. The first is a decider who has the information. The second is consistency between the decisions of many teams, since each team decides from its own part. The third is a path by which the information that the next decision needs reaches the person who will make it.

The common view is that a shared culture and frequent communication supply these three unaided, so that process is only a substitute for trust. That view falls short in one place. Informal coordination covers the paths between people who already talk. It leaves out the paths that the organization needs and nobody walks: three teams with no reason to meet can build the same thing, each in good faith. Asking such teams to align tells them nothing about whom to ask or who decides. So the three missing things do not arise from goodwill; they have to be designed.

## Three Mechanisms of Alignment

Part VI supplies the three missing things with three mechanisms, one per chapter and in the same order:

- **Decision rights and decision records.** A **decision right**, in the definition of [[the-organization-as-a-system]], is the agreed answer to who may make a named kind of decision, whose input they must take and where the decision is written down. It puts the decision with the role that has the information. A **decision record** states the options, the reasons and the conditions for revisiting the decision, so that the next decision can build on it.
- **Planning.** A plan makes the decisions of many teams consistent in advance. It is made in three steps, in order and separately, which follow Will Larson's planning model in *The Engineering Executive's Primer*. The **financial plan** comes before the two steps named in the introduction and fixes how much the organization can spend. **Allocation** assigns capacity, which means people, to areas of work and holds it steady. The **choice of outcomes** comes third: each area chooses, from the roadmap of Part III, the outcomes its capacity will pursue. **Team objectives** join allocation to outcomes: they record, as a contract between leaders and teams, which outcomes each team's capacity will pursue.
- **Operating cadence.** The cadence is the small set of recurring **forums**, meetings with a stated purpose, and **written artefacts**, documents such as a written plan or a log of decisions, that moves information to where the next decision is made. Each forum has an owner, a written input and a decision or a flow of information that it exists for.

The three mechanisms depend on each other in one direction. Decision rights come first because planning is a set of decisions and has to know who makes each one. The cadence comes last because its forums exist to make those decisions and to carry those plans; a cadence designed first is a calendar of meetings with nothing to decide. Each mechanism is also designed and judged under the two lenses through which this book looks at organizations, system design and product thinking. As system design, each is a path laid out on purpose for one of the three flows that Part I found in every organization: a decision right for decisions, the cadence for information, and the plan for work, because it says which team's capacity goes to which outcome. As product thinking, each has users, the people who decide and the teams that wait for a decision, and it is kept only while it serves them; a forum that no longer serves a decision is cancelled. The middle chapter is also where two earlier parts meet. Part III's roadmap says what capacity is for, Part IV's engineering work needs capacity too, and allocation says how much goes where.

## Chapters in this part

Each of the three chapters answers one question:

- [[decision-rights-and-decision-records]] — By what method are decision rights allocated, and how is a decision recorded so that it lasts?
- [[planning]] — How do capacity planning and outcome planning fit into one process?
- [[operating-cadence]] — By what rule does an organization decide which recurring forums and written artefacts it needs as it grows, and what is the smallest set at each size?

Read in that order, the three chapters follow a decision from who makes it, through the plan that many such decisions add up to, to the rhythm of meetings and documents in which decisions and plans are made and carried. At Corvane nobody had said which decisions the nine weeks were for, so the planning could run long and still leave three teams on the same integration. With the three answers, a leader who finds teams pulling apart looks for the missing decision right, the planning steps tangled into one or the absent forum before calling for better communication. If you arrive with one question about decisions, planning or meetings, open the chapter that answers it; otherwise read in order. So far the claim is that alignment needs explicit mechanisms.
