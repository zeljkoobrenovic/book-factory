> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **Two leaders can draw the same organization in two ways.** In 2025 Corvane's CTO and its new CPO disagreed about who led eleven of the teams, and nothing said which drawing was right.
> * **A leader's distinctive work is joining decisions.** Roles and layers follow from where decisions have to be made together, and each one has to earn the delay it adds.
> * **Decisions are joined in four places.** At the top, across product, engineering and design, down the management line, and across teams on technical questions.

<br>

In 2025 Corvane appointed its first Chief Product Officer (CPO). Corvane is the fictional company this book follows; that year it was growing from 180 people toward 500. The new CPO was Priya Nambiar. She and Tomas Rell, co-founder and Chief Technology Officer (CTO), each drew the organization they needed. **The two drawings disagreed about who led eleven of the teams.**

You have probably seen such a disagreement settled around people. A role was shaped for the person who was available, or a title was created for someone who had to be kept. One usual view says structure is a matter of people: give strong leaders scope and titles that keep them. The other says it is a matter of template: a company of this size has these roles. Both skip a question. **What is each role for?** Without an answer, Corvane's two drawings could be compared only by who had drawn them. Each could be defended by the strengths of the people on it, and neither said what a leader of those eleven teams would decide that nobody else could.

Part V answers that question. It argues that **leadership structure is derived, and not chosen from a template**. Executive roles follow from which decisions must be joined, that is, made together, at the top. The **triad**, in which product, engineering and design decide together, repeats at every level where those decisions recur. **Spans**, the number of people each manager leads, and **layers**, the levels of management between the executives and the teams, follow from team size. And a technical leadership track carries the decisions that the management structure cannot. This page first says what a leader is for, and then gives the four places where decisions are joined. Four chapters then answer four questions in order: which executive roles are needed and who reports to whom, how product, engineering and design decide together, how many people a manager leads and how many layers follow, and what senior technical leaders own.

## What a Leader Is For

Why can the structure be derived? Because of what a leader does that nobody else is placed to do. To **join** two decisions is to make them in one place, so that each takes account of the other. A team decides with its own information; it cannot make its decisions together with those of another team or another function, or with next year's. That is a leader's distinctive work: **joining decisions that would otherwise be made separately**, product with engineering, one group of teams with another, this quarter with next year. The team boundaries of Part II, the product model of Part III and the engineering model of Part IV already say which decisions fall between teams and between functions. So the leadership structure can be derived from those three designs.

Joining has a cost, however. It takes **judgment**, which [[the-organization-as-a-system]] defined as deciding with the relevant information at hand, and judgment is scarce. Every layer of leaders also adds delay and distance from the information. So each role and each layer has to pass a test: it joins decisions that the units below it cannot join themselves. Without the test, joining decisions would justify any number of leaders. The test corresponds to the two lenses through which this book looks at organizations, system design and product thinking. Saying where a decision is joined is system design: it places the decision at one level and not at another. Judging a role or a layer by what it does for the teams below it is product thinking: those teams are its users, and the measure is how long they wait for a decision that only it can make.

## Four Places Where Decisions Are Joined

Part V applies the test in four places, one per chapter:

- **At the top: executive roles.** Someone is accountable for what is built, someone for how it is built and run, and there is a place where the two are joined. There are three common configurations, and each trades one kind of joining for another. A CTO and a CPO as peers keep what is built and how it is built in balance and join them slowly, by negotiation. One executive who holds both joins them fast, in one head, and leans to one side. A CTO with a VP Engineering splits engineering in two, technical direction and the running of the organization, and suits a company where joining those two is the hardest.
- **Across functions: the triad.** Product, engineering and design decide together at team, group and executive level. Each is accountable for one risk: product for whether the thing is worth building, engineering for whether it can be built and run, and design for whether people can use it.
- **Down the line: spans and layers.** A line manager leads one team, so the first span is the team size of Part II. A **manager of managers** leads about four to six managers, which is Will Larson's rule of thumb in *An Elegant Puzzle*. The number of layers follows by arithmetic from headcount and those spans, and a layer is kept only if it passes the test.
- **Across teams: the technical leadership track.** Two kinds of senior technical role, **staff-plus** engineers (staff and principal engineers) and architects, hold the technical decisions that cross teams. A manager joins the decisions of the teams that report to them. A technical decision often crosses teams that report to different managers, and it needs someone with the technical depth to be believed by all of them. The track works when each of its roles has a stated scope and accountability.

The four places are read from the top down, because each builds on what the earlier ones settle. The executive level comes first because its configuration decides where product and engineering meet. The triad comes next because it is that same meeting, with design, repeated at each level below. Spans and layers come third because they say how many levels there are; start with them, and the layers are counted before anyone knows what the top layer is. The technical track comes last because it is defined by what the management line leaves unowned. Every chapter carries both lenses: each says where a decision is joined, and each names the people a role or a layer serves and the measure that judges it. The part also draws on two earlier ones. Spans start from the team sizes of [[team-size-and-composition]] in Part II, and the technical track holds the senior engineers on whom the governance of Part IV, in [[architecture-governance]], already relied.

Each of the four chapters answers one question:

- [[executive-roles-and-reporting-lines]] — Which executive roles does a product and tech organization need, and who should report to whom?

Read in that order, the four chapters build a leadership structure from the top down and then across teams. At Corvane the CTO and the CPO argued about who should lead the eleven teams, and nobody had asked what each role was for, which is why both drawings could be defended and neither could be shown to be right. With the four answers, a leader can explain each role and each layer by the decisions it joins, and can remove or decline one that joins none. If you arrive with one question about leadership structure, open the chapter that answers it; otherwise read in order. So far the claim is that leadership structure is derived from the decisions that must be joined. The first chapter takes the executive level, where the first such joining is made or missed: [[executive-roles-and-reporting-lines]].
