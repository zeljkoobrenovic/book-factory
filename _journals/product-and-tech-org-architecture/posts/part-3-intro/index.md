> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **A list of requests cannot steer.** A request names a solution, and a list of solutions holds no reason to prefer one item to another or to stop.
> * **Four steps replace the list.** An outcome to aim at, a vision and a strategy that put outcomes in order, a roadmap that commits only as far as its evidence reaches, and a stated division of decisions.
> * **Leaders own the problems, and teams own the solutions.** Direction from the top and decisions in teams stop conflicting once that line is written down.

<br>

In 2024, at 180 people, Corvane shipped forty-one features. Corvane is the fictional company this book follows. Each feature had been promised to a customer by name, and the roadmap was the list of those promises with their dates. That year the **renewal rate**, the share of customers due for renewal who renewed, fell. Corvane built what its customers asked for, **and more of them left**.

You have probably worked to a roadmap like this one. It was delivered, and nothing that mattered moved. The usual explanations are wrong features, late delivery, or sales promising too much. One other question explains more. **What was the roadmap for?** Corvane's list could not say. Nothing on it said what should be different for a customer once a feature shipped, so the list could be finished without anyone knowing whether it had worked.

Part III's answer is that a roadmap is for outcomes, and it says what has to be in place before a roadmap can state them. It argues that an organization delivers **outcomes**, results its customers can observe, and not only **output**, what it ships, when a vision of the customer's future bounds the problems worth solving, a strategy puts those problems in order, the roadmap states outcomes only as far ahead as there is evidence for, and teams own the solutions. Setting direction at the top and letting teams decide look like opposites, and Part III also argues that they do not conflict once the line between a problem and its solution is explicit. This page first says why a list of requests cannot steer an organization, and then gives the four steps that replace the list. Four chapters then answer four questions in order: what to aim at instead of output, how a vision becomes a strategy, what a roadmap commits to, and what a team decides for itself.

## Why a List of Requests Cannot Steer

The reason a delivered roadmap can change nothing lies in what a request is. An outcome is a change a customer can observe in their own work, such as a planner who no longer replans the day by phone when a technician calls in sick. A customer's request names a solution, so a list of requests is a list of outputs. Such a list lacks two reasons. It holds **no reason to prefer** one item to another, so the order comes from outside it, usually from who asked loudest. And it holds **no reason to stop**: when an item ships, all the list can say is that the next one is due.

What supplies both reasons is an outcome with a vision behind it. A **vision**, in this part, is a description of the customer's future experience with the product. A stated outcome is the reason to prefer: a team can compare one solution with another by what each would do for the result. It is also the reason to stop, and it gives leaders a way to judge: when the result is reached the work is done, and a feature that shipped and moved nothing shows up as a cost. The vision says which problems are worth solving, and so which outcomes matter. Neither the outcome nor the vision names the solution. That choice stays with the people closest to the customer and the code.

## Four Steps from Outcome to Decision

Part III gets from a list of requests to an outcome with a vision behind it, and to teams that choose the solutions, in four steps, one per chapter. Most of the terms come from *Build What Matters*, by Ben Foster and Rajesh Nerlikar:

- **An outcome to aim at.** The unit of progress is a **key customer outcome**, the result the customer wants in the customer's own terms, paired with a **key business outcome** that follows from it, such as renewals.
- **A vision and a strategy.** The **customer journey vision** describes the customer's future experience from the customer's side. The **product strategy** works backward from it: the few steps, in order, that lead from today's journey to that one.
- **A roadmap with horizons.** The roadmap lays the strategy out in time. A **horizon** is how far ahead an item sits; the further ahead, the less the roadmap can honestly promise. The roadmap commits to outcomes. It also gives a deliberate share of capacity to each of three kinds of work, so that urgent work does not take all of it: innovation, which moves toward the vision; iteration, which improves what is already built; and operation, which keeps the product running.
- **A division of decisions.** Leaders own the **problem space**: which problems matter and in what order. Teams own the **solution space**: what to build and how it should work. Three things let a team use its share: **discovery**, finding out which solution works before building it in full; delivery; and **product operations**, the support that gives teams data and access to customers.

The four steps depend on each other in one direction. A vision and a strategy are stated in terms of outcomes, so outcomes come first. The roadmap is the strategy laid out in time, so it comes third. The division of decisions comes last because it needs the other three: hand a team the choice of solution before any outcome is stated, and the team has nothing to decide for.

The last step holds the apparent conflict that this page's introduction pointed to. If leaders set a vision, teams seem to be left with execution; if teams decide, a vision seems to stand in their way. The conflict goes away once the line between the problem space and the solution space is written down. The vision bounds the problems and leaves the solutions open, so each level decides where it has the information: leaders see across teams and across time, and teams see the customer and the code. This sets Part III against two common views: that product work is collecting customers' requests and delivering them on time, and that any direction from the top only constrains teams.

The four steps also carry the two lenses through which this book looks at organizations, system design and product thinking. The outcome, the roadmap and discovery are product thinking: [[the-organization-as-a-product]] took them from product work and applied them to the organization itself, and Part III presents them where they come from, in the product built for customers. The division of decisions is system design: each level deciding where it has the information is a **decision right**, the lever from Part I that says who decides what, applied to product work.

## Chapters in this part

Each of the four chapters answers one question:

- [[outcomes-over-output]] — Why do organizations that ship a great deal still fail their customers, and what should they aim at instead?
- [[vision-and-product-strategy]] — How does a product vision become a strategy that teams can act on?
- [[roadmaps-and-horizons]] — What should a roadmap commit to, and how far ahead can it honestly look?
- [[discovery-delivery-and-empowered-teams]] — What may a product team decide for itself, and how does that fit with a vision set at the top?

The four chapters put four answers in the place of one list: what to aim at, in which order, by when and with how much certainty, and who chooses the solution. Corvane's list of forty-one promises said what would ship, for whom and when, and it gave none of the four. With the four answers, a leader stops approving feature lists and starts stating outcomes and their order, and a team stops asking what to build and starts answering for a result. With one question about product work, open the chapter that answers it; otherwise read in order. So far the claim is that an organization should deliver outcomes and not output. The first chapter shows what organizations driven by output look like, and says what an outcome is: [[outcomes-over-output]].
