> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **Payments go wrong at hand-offs.** In four payments followed end to end, every failure appears where one step's answer is taken by the next system as final.
> * **A success message reports a promise.** Whether it was kept is reported later, in another record, so a payment's record stays open until the dispute window closes, not just until the payout.
> * **Each provider puts the later record somewhere else.** Stripe gives objects whose status can be read again; Adyen gives events and report lines keyed by a reference.

<br>
At the end of 2025, every payment in the records of **Larkfield, the fictional studio-software platform this book follows**, had the right status. Yet its books said it owed its studios €412,000 more than the balances at its two providers, Stripe and Adyen, showed. The difference was found in January 2026 and took three weeks to trace: to refunds made after a studio had been paid, fees on chargebacks (card payments taken back by the customer's bank) and a rounding rule for currency conversion ([[payment-ledgers]] gives the amounts). **None of the three was a payment that had failed.**

**Figure 1:** *At year-end 2025 every payment at Larkfield had the right status, and its books and its providers' balances were €412,000 apart.*

The chapters so far could not have shown that difference. Parts I to VIII explained one mechanism per chapter, ending in [[part-8]] with the rule that a money path must be correct before it is available: an answer may be late, but the record may not be wrong. Part IX teaches no new mechanism. It takes **four payments at Studio Noord, a yoga studio that uses Larkfield, and follows each through every party and system, in the order the payment meets them**, to show what passes from each step to the next.

The four make one claim: **every failure they show appears at a hand-off**, the point where one mechanism's answer (an approval, a decline, a payout, a payment marked complete) is taken by the next system as final. The mechanisms belong to different parties and run on different clocks, from seconds for an approval to about four months for a chargeback. So each step can tell the next only what it knows when it finishes, which is a promise. Whether the promise was kept is reported later, by another record, usually on another channel; a card payment is final only when the dispute window closes.

**Figure 2:** *The shape of Part IX: four payments, each a chain of mechanisms that work, read at the joints where one hands to the next.*

Every journey has the same form: a narrative, one sequence figure and a **step table**, which the first journey sets up. Each row names the party or system that acts, the record it leaves, the promise that record makes and what can fail there, with the chapter that owns the mechanism. Read a row by asking **which later record confirms or corrects it**. Each journey is told on Adyen, Studio Noord's provider, with the Stripe record beside it. The two report the same step through different records at different moments: Stripe as objects whose status can be read again, Adyen as events and report lines keyed by a reference. An integration that waits for the right record on one waits for the wrong one on the other.

The four journeys are read in the order of what each adds, not the calendar's. The disputed payment was made first, on 2 February 2027, five weeks before Mila's class pass of 9 March 2027. It comes last because a chargeback debits a balance, and you should first see that balance filled, paid out and refunded.

## Chapters in this part

[[journey-card-payment]] asks **what happens to one €120 card payment between the tap and the studio's reconciled payout, and what each step promises**. Mila Janssen buys a ten-class pass at 19:42 on Tuesday 9 March 2027, and Studio Noord is paid on Thursday 11 March. Nothing fails: it is the baseline. Of its twelve steps, the first eleven each end in a promise. The “Payment successful” on her screen reports the promise of the fifth step. Only the twelfth, matching the payout to the provider's settlement record, establishes that the studio's share arrived: €117.47, the €120 less Larkfield's €2.53. On Stripe the payment can be read again; Adyen's Checkout API has no call that returns it, and a capture reported as successful can fail days later.

[[journey-failed-renewal]] keeps the member and her stored card, but Mila is not there to pay and the first attempt is declined. It asks **who decides whether and when to try again, and which system holds the membership until the money arrives**. Mila's €49 renewal is declined on 1 April 2027 and collected on 4 April; a second branch follows a renewal that is never collected. A declined renewal is an open debt with a clock: the decline code and Visa's rules of 24 October 2026 decide whether and how often to retry, and no payment record says what Mila is in between. Stripe Billing retries an invoice it holds. On Adyen the provider can retry a payment, and the schedule, the membership state and the notice are Larkfield's.

[[journey-platform-payment]] opens the pass's split and payout rows to ask **how the €120 is divided, and whose money pays a refund after the studio has been paid**. An instruction attached to the payment divides it into €117.47 for the studio and €2.53 for Larkfield. On 18 March, a week after the payout, Mila gets €84 back, debited from a balance the payout had emptied and so paid from other members' later payments. The account model chosen at integration decides who goes short: on Adyen the platform's own balance account stands behind the studio's, and on Stripe the charge type decides whose balance is debited first.

[[journey-dispute]] turns from money that goes back by agreement to money a bank takes back, and asks **what a chargeback costs in money, deadlines and records, and who carries the loss**. Bram de Wit, another member, paid €120 on 2 February, and his bank charged it back on 24 February. The €120 and an illustrative €15 fee left the studio's balance before anyone had read any evidence. Larkfield could answer once, within the 40 days Adyen gives for a Mastercard. The money came back provisionally on 5 March and the dispute was won on 12 May, but the card network's monitoring counted the dispute whatever its outcome. Who paid had been decided when the payment was made: on Adyen by the chargeback logic set on the payment, on Stripe by the charge type. The journey ends in Larkfield's ledger and closes the part.

The journeys quote the providers' documentation as read in November 2026, and each says where that documentation stops. The pairing of the two vocabularies is this book's own, since neither company publishes one. The timing of clearing and settlement between banks is industry practice, not a cited rule. Mastercard's rule documents could not be read, and Adyen's chargeback fee is set by contract, so the dispute journey's €15 fee is an illustration.

## Where to Begin

Read the four as a set, or start at the one that matches a payment, renewal, refund or dispute you are tracing, and fill in the same table. Read through, they turn the €412,000 into three kinds of later record that reached a provider's balance and never Larkfield's books; the third and fourth journeys write two of them (refunds after payout, chargeback fees) as ledger entries; the rounding rule belongs to [[payment-ledgers]]. When a payment of your own goes wrong, look first for the hand-off where a promise was taken for a fact, and for the later record that would have shown the difference. Begin with [[journey-card-payment]], the class pass on which nothing fails.
