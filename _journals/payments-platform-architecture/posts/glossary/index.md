> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Look up any term the book uses, read its definition beside the terms it is confused with, and translate it between Stripe's and Adyen's vocabularies.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **One term, one meaning, one owning chapter.** Each row gives a definition of one or two sentences in the book's own neutral wording and links to the chapter that owns the term. The definitions follow those chapters; when a chapter and this page disagree, one of them needs correcting.
> * **Translate by definition, not by spelling.** Stripe and Adyen often use different words for one thing (application fee and commission), the same word for different things (reserve, transfer, available, chargeback), and sometimes divide a concept differently: one connected account at Stripe is a legal entity, an account holder, a balance account and a transfer instrument at Adyen.
> * **Every entry is dated.** Provider terms are as read in the two companies' documentation in November 2026, legal entries summarize a rule and defer to its source, and “no counterpart found” is a search result, not a statement by the provider. Larkfield and its names are fiction.

<br>

At Stripe a **reserve** is money the provider holds back from a merchant against refunds and chargebacks. At Adyen the reserve is a buffer the merchant sets for itself, and the provider's hold is called a deposit ([[balances-and-funds-flows]]). The two companies often use different words for one concept, sometimes the same word for two, and sometimes divide a concept differently, so a reader moving between their documentation has to translate **by definition, not by spelling**. This page therefore gives each term one definition in the book's own neutral wording and one owning chapter, and sets beside it, in a crosswalk, the word each provider uses and a note where the two do not match. For some terms, such as the unit that represents a seller on a platform, no one-to-one translation exists.

The page has four parts, in this order. First come the definitions, grouped by the book's parts, each with a link to the chapter that owns the term. Then comes the Stripe–Adyen vocabulary crosswalk, one table per area. After it are the names of Larkfield, the fictional studio-software platform this book follows, and last an alphabetical index of everything. The material is the chapters' own definitions and the two providers' documentation as read in November 2026. The previous page, [[toolkit]], uses terms such as idempotency key, clearing account, payout return and PCI scope without defining them; they are defined here.

## How to Use This Glossary

Browse by the book's parts, or jump to the **[alphabetical index](#g-index)** for a term, an alias, an abbreviation or a provider's own word. To translate between the two providers, go to the **[crosswalk](#g-crosswalk)**: its tables are grouped by area, and the index marks every provider term with its company.

The **292 definition rows** are short teaching definitions, and the **115 crosswalk rows** each name one concept in both vocabularies. The last column links to the chapter that owns the term (the first link) and to others that use it; the fuller explanation, the worked example and the dated sources are there. Terms separated by a slash are defined together to show how they differ, and acronyms are expanded in the row.

Three kinds of entry need care. **Larkfield is fictional**, and its names and figures illustrate the definitions ([[meet-larkfield]]). **Legal and standards entries** summarize a rule as of November 2026; consult the cited source for the requirement itself. And **a provider's term** is the word its documentation used when the chapter was written: both companies rename things, so check the current page before a word from this table goes into a column name, a contract or a design decision.

## <a id="g-money"></a>Part I — MONEY: How a Payment Actually Moves

Part I's terms name the parties to a card payment and the steps between “pay” and “paid”: the approval, the claim, the record, the money, the routes back and the fees. Read *authorization*, *capture*, *clearing*, *settlement* and *payout* as five different events with five different owners ([[journey-of-a-payment]]).

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Four-party model** | The standard picture of a card payment: the cardholder; the issuer, the cardholder's bank; the merchant, who sells; and the acquirer, the institution on the merchant's side. The card network stands between the two institutions, which makes five parties. | [[journey-of-a-payment]] |
| **Issuer** | The bank that gave the cardholder the card and keeps the account behind it. It decides every authorization, and it is the party a cardholder complains to in a dispute. | [[journey-of-a-payment]], [[payment-authorization]] |
| **Acquirer** | The licensed institution that accepts card payments on the merchant's behalf: it submits them to the card network as a member and is the first party to receive the money for them. | [[acquiring-and-network-connectivity]], [[journey-of-a-payment]] |
| **Card network** | Visa, Mastercard or another network that carries the authorization request between acquirer and issuer, sets the rules and runs the daily settlement between its member banks. Adyen's documentation says “scheme”. | [[journey-of-a-payment]] |
| **Merchant** | The party that sells and that the cardholder buys from. On a platform the card networks' rules decide who that is; see sponsored merchant and merchant of record. | [[journey-of-a-payment]], [[platform-payment-models]] |
| **Payment service provider (PSP)** | A company that packages some or all of gateway, processor, payment facilitator and acquirer behind one contract and one API; whether it is itself the acquirer depends on provider and country. The book says “the provider”, and “payment provider” in the preface. | [[journey-of-a-payment]], [[how-payments-got-complicated]] |
| **Payment facilitator (PayFac)** | An entity that signs many small merchants under one acquirer and pays them out; in Visa's rules it contracts with an acquirer “on behalf of a Sponsored Merchant”. Its acquirer registers it with the network and stays liable for it. | [[journey-of-a-payment]], [[platform-payment-models]] |
| **Platform** | Software through which other businesses sell and get paid. It holds funds only if it is licensed to; otherwise the money sits with a licensed provider. | [[journey-of-a-payment]], [[platform-payment-models]] |
| **Three-party network** | A card network in which one company is both issuer and acquirer; Stripe's documentation gives American Express as the example of such a standalone network. | [[journey-of-a-payment]] |
| **Bank-transfer scheme** | A way of paying in which the payer's bank and the merchant's bank deal with each other under the scheme's rules: the money is pushed by the payer or pulled by the merchant under a mandate. | [[journey-of-a-payment]], [[local-payment-methods]] |
| **Wallet** | Two different things under one name: a saved payment credential, such as a tokenized card held on a phone, which changes how the card is presented and not the parties; or a stored balance held with the wallet provider. | [[journey-of-a-payment]], [[network-tokens]] |
| **A message, then a record, then money** | The fixed order in which three different things move in a card payment: the authorization is a message, clearing is a record, and settlement and payout are money. A status reports one of them, decided by one party. | [[journey-of-a-payment]] |
| **Authorization** | The promise of the cardholder's bank to honor a claim for up to an approved amount inside a limited window. It moves no money; before the claim arrives it can lapse, shrink or be withdrawn. | [[payment-authorization]] |
| **Hold** | What an approving issuer places on the cardholder's account: it lowers the amount the cardholder can spend by the approved amount without debiting the account. | [[payment-authorization]], [[journey-of-a-payment]] |
| **Response code** | The issuer's answer to an authorization request: a two-character code in the response message. In practice it is usually uninformative (“05: Do not honor”), so providers add their own normalized reason and the network's retry advice. | [[payment-authorization]] |
| **Authorization validity window** | The time within which an approved payment must be captured. For a customer-initiated online Visa payment, Visa's rule and Adyen's page give 10 days and Stripe's page gives 7; a merchant-initiated Visa payment has 5. | [[payment-authorization]] |
| **Final authorization / pre-authorization / incremental authorization / partial authorization** | A final authorization is for the exact amount and is the default on both providers; a pre-authorization is for an estimate; an incremental authorization raises an approved estimate; a partial authorization is the issuer approving less than was asked. | [[payment-authorization]] |
| **Zero-value authorization** | An authorization that reserves nothing, sent to confirm that a card can be used before it is stored; Visa calls it an Account Verification. | [[payment-authorization]] |
| **Reversal (authorization reversal)** | Giving the promise back: a message that cancels an approval before capture, so no payment appears on the cardholder's statement. Often called a cancel or a void; the word “reversal” shifts by provider (see the crosswalk). | [[payment-authorization]], [[refunds-reversals-and-disputes]] |
| **Customer-initiated transaction (CIT) / merchant-initiated transaction (MIT)** | A CIT is a payment the cardholder takes part in. An MIT is one the merchant starts from stored details while the cardholder is away, on the strength of a prior agreement; the label changes which rules, validity window and authentication duty apply. | [[payment-authorization]], [[three-d-secure-and-sca]] |
| **Stored credential** | Card information kept by a merchant, its agent, a payment facilitator or a wallet operator to process future transactions. Visa's rules require the cardholder's agreement and an approved authorization or account verification before it is stored. | [[payment-authorization]] |
| **Stand-in processing (STIP)** | The network answering an authorization on the issuer's behalf when the issuer is unavailable, too slow or has asked for it. Whether a given approval came from stand-in is not public for either provider. | [[payment-authorization]] |
| **ISO 8583** | The card industry's message standard, in which an authorization request travels from the acquirer through the network to the issuer. | [[payment-authorization]] |
| **Capture** | The merchant's claim on an authorization: the instruction to collect the approved amount, sent automatically by default on both providers or later by the merchant (manual, delayed, partial or multiple capture). | [[clearing-and-settlement]] |
| **Clearing** | The exchange of records between acquirer and issuer through the network that fixes the final amount and data of a captured payment. It is a record, not money. | [[clearing-and-settlement]], [[journey-of-a-payment]] |
| **Presentment** | In Visa's rules, “A Clearing Record that an Acquirer presents to an Issuer through Interchange”: the final statement of what was sold and for how much. In the rulebook “Interchange” means this exchange of records, not the fee. | [[clearing-and-settlement]] |
| **Dual-message / single-message network** | A dual-message network uses separate messages to authorize and to clear a transaction, as online card payments typically do; a single-message network does both in one message and normally carries PIN-authenticated debit. | [[clearing-and-settlement]] |
| **Net settlement** | The network's daily transfer of money between its member banks, as one net amount per member, on the next business day or later. The networks' cut-off times and number of settlement cycles a day are not public. | [[clearing-and-settlement]] |
| **“Settled” (three meanings)** | At the network: the daily net amount has reached the acquirer. At the provider: funds are released to the merchant's balance or batch. At the merchant's bank: the payout has arrived. A payout date does not say when the banks settled. | [[clearing-and-settlement]], [[journey-of-a-payment]] |
| **Funding (pass-through, fixed-delay, instant)** | The provider's own step of making a sale's money payable to the merchant, on the provider's schedule: pass-through funding pays as each method's funds arrive; fixed-delay funding pays a whole day's sales after a set number of days; instant funding removes the delay for a fee. | [[clearing-and-settlement]], [[balances-and-funds-flows]] |
| **Refund** | Money sent back to the customer at the merchant's request after capture. It is a new transaction that can itself fail, not an undo of the first. | [[refunds-reversals-and-disputes]] |
| **Dispute / chargeback** | A dispute is a cardholder's complaint to the issuer that a charge should be reversed; the chargeback is the forced return of the money through the network. The merchant does not start it, and it runs on the issuer's clock whatever has been settled and paid out. | [[refunds-reversals-and-disputes]] |
| **Reason code** | The ground an issuer gives for a dispute. The grounds fall into four families, which Visa numbers as categories 10 to 13; the family decides what evidence could answer the claim. | [[refunds-reversals-and-disputes]] |
| **Representment / pre-arbitration / arbitration** | Representment is the merchant's side presenting the transaction again with evidence (Visa now calls it a Dispute Response); pre-arbitration is the last exchange between the two banks; arbitration is the network's ruling. | [[refunds-reversals-and-disputes]], [[journey-dispute]] |
| **Dispute window** | The time in which a card payment can still be disputed: about 120 days after the sale under Visa's public rules, sometimes longer. Until it closes, nothing is final. | [[refunds-reversals-and-disputes]], [[journey-of-a-payment]] |
| **Dispute monitoring program** | A network scheme that counts a merchant's fraud reports and disputes against its sales each month and fines or restricts those above a threshold; Visa's is the Visa Acquirer Monitoring Program (VAMP). | [[refunds-reversals-and-disputes]] |
| **Rapid Dispute Resolution** | Visa's pre-dispute service that lets merchants and payment facilitators automate accepting liability for a disputed transaction; the case is resolved through a refund and does not become a chargeback. | [[refunds-reversals-and-disputes]] |
| **Friendly fraud** | A legitimate cardholder makes a purchase and disputes it later, by accident or on purpose (also “first-party misuse”). It arrives under the same fraud code as a dispute over a stolen card. | [[refunds-reversals-and-disputes]], [[payment-fraud-and-risk]] |
| **Merchant discount rate (MDR)** | The total a merchant pays to accept a card payment: the sum of interchange, scheme fees and the acquirer's or provider's markup. | [[payment-economics]] |
| **Interchange** | The fee the cardholder's bank (the issuer) receives on each card payment, set by the network as a default and capped by law for some cards. Not to be confused with the rulebook's “Interchange”, the exchange of clearing records. | [[payment-economics]] |
| **Scheme fee** | What the card network charges on a payment, by value, number of messages or crossing a border. The kinds are public; the fee schedules are not. | [[payment-economics]] |
| **Markup** | The part of the fee the acquirer or provider keeps for itself, set by contract. | [[payment-economics]] |
| **Blended pricing** | One percentage plus a fixed amount for every payment of a kind, whatever the card behind it costs the provider. | [[payment-economics]] |
| **Interchange-plus-plus pricing (IC++)** | Pass-through pricing: the merchant pays the actual interchange and scheme fee of each payment plus the provider's fixed markup, so the provider's share is fixed and the total varies. Never compare a blended rate with a markup. | [[payment-economics]] |
| **Net revenue / take rate** | Net revenue is what a provider keeps after passing interchange and scheme fees on; take rate is net revenue divided by the value of payments processed, usually in basis points (one basis point is 0.01%). Company-reported volume is not revenue. | [[payment-economics]], [[case-adyen]] |
| **Interchange Fee Regulation** | Regulation (EU) 2015/751, which caps interchange on consumer cards of four-party networks at 0.2% of the value for debit and 0.3% for credit. | [[payment-economics]] |
| **Durbin amendment / Regulation II** | The US statute and the Federal Reserve regulation that cap debit interchange for covered issuers ($0.21 plus 0.05% of the value, plus a $0.01 fraud-prevention adjustment) and forbid restricting a debit card to fewer than two unaffiliated networks, which is what allows least-cost routing. | [[payment-economics]], [[payment-routing-and-orchestration]] |

## <a id="g-acceptance"></a>Part II — ACCEPTANCE: Getting to Yes

Part II's terms cover the provider's side of the authorization message: who may send it and under which license, what “yes” means for a method that is not a card, how a payment is modeled as states, and what changes when several providers or a terminal are involved. Provider-specific names such as PaymentIntent and modification are in the [crosswalk](#g-x-payments).

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Acquirer of record** | This book's name for the entity that holds the acquirer's role for a given payment. The question to put to a provider first: which legal entity is the acquirer for these sellers, in this country? | [[acquiring-and-network-connectivity]] |
| **Principal member / associate member** | Tiers of card-network membership. In Visa's rules a principal-type member licenses its own network identifiers and is liable for all activity on them; an associate-type member uses an identifier licensed to its sponsor, which is liable where the associate cannot meet its responsibility. | [[acquiring-and-network-connectivity]] |
| **BIN / acquiring identifier** | The network identifiers a member licenses: a bank identification number (BIN) for issuing and an acquiring identifier for acquiring. Liability follows the identifier. | [[acquiring-and-network-connectivity]] |
| **Sponsor bank** | The acquiring bank under whose network membership a payment facilitator or another non-member signs up merchants; the bank stays liable to the network for them. | [[acquiring-and-network-connectivity]], [[licences-and-safeguarding]] |
| **Processor / gateway** | A processor (acquirer processor) runs the technical connection and message processing for an acquirer; a gateway carries payment data from the merchant to an acquirer or processor. Neither holds the membership or the money. | [[acquiring-and-network-connectivity]], [[journey-of-a-payment]] |
| **Local acquiring** | Having an acquirer whose license reaches the country of the sale, so that the business sells there as a local merchant and that country's cards do not arrive at their issuers as international payments. | [[acquiring-and-network-connectivity]] |
| **MID (merchant ID)** | The account number an acquirer gives a merchant. | [[payment-routing-and-orchestration]] |
| **Payment method** | A way of paying that runs on a scheme: a body of rules plus the banks and companies that follow them. (In this sense “scheme” is wider than Adyen's use of the word for a card network.) | [[local-payment-methods]] |
| **Payment method family** | A group of payment methods that behave alike after `paid`: cards, bank debits, bank redirects, bank transfers, wallets, buy now pay later, real-time payments, vouchers. The family, not the brand, is the unit to design for: keep one policy per family. | [[local-payment-methods]] |
| **Push / pull** | In a push the payer instructs their own bank to send money; in a pull the merchant instructs the payer's bank to take money under a permission given earlier. | [[local-payment-methods]] |
| **Account-to-account payment** | A payment that moves money between two bank accounts with no card network in between. | [[local-payment-methods]], [[payments-what-is-moving]] |
| **Finality** | The point after which the payer can no longer take a payment back through their bank. It differs by method: an iDEAL payment cannot be disputed with the payer's bank, while a SEPA direct debit can be disputed without a reason for eight weeks. | [[local-payment-methods]] |
| **Mandate** | The payer's standing permission for a named creditor to debit their account: a record somebody must be able to produce years later. | [[local-payment-methods]] |
| **Direct debit** | A pull from the payer's bank account under a mandate, which can be returned weeks later. SEPA Direct Debit is the scheme of the Single Euro Payments Area; Bacs Direct Debit the British one; ACH the US one. | [[local-payment-methods]], [[invoicing-collections-and-dunning]] |
| **Redirect flow** | A checkout in which the customer leaves the merchant's page to approve the payment at their bank, and may not come back. iDEAL (the Netherlands; being replaced by Wero) and Bancontact (Belgium) work this way. | [[local-payment-methods]] |
| **Charge-request model** | The older model in which a payment is one server call that carries the card details and returns the outcome, and the merchant's record is that answer. It broke on payments that need a customer action or finish later. | [[payment-state-machines]] |
| **Payment state machine** | A record created on the server before any money moves, which holds a state, the transitions allowed from that state and every attempt made to pay. It must guarantee one terminal outcome, legal transitions and idempotent transitions. | [[payment-state-machines]] |
| **Next action** | The provider's instruction to the client about what the customer must do before the payment can go on: a redirect, a 3-D Secure challenge, a voucher to pay. | [[payment-state-machines]] |
| **Authorization rate / payment success rate / checkout conversion** | Three rates with three denominators. Stripe's authorization rate divides payments authorized by the issuer by unique attempts submitted to the network; payment success rate divides them by attempts submitted through the provider; checkout conversion is completed payments over checkouts started. | [[authorization-optimization]] |
| **Blocked payment** | A payment the provider stops itself before it reaches the card network, as a risk block or to save a fee; it leaves the authorization-rate denominator. | [[authorization-optimization]], [[payment-fraud-and-risk]] |
| **Soft decline / hard decline** | A soft decline is a refusal that might become an approval later or with corrected data; a hard decline will not change for this card. Both are industry shorthand, not network terms: the network vocabulary is Visa's decline-code categories and Mastercard's merchant advice code. | [[authorization-optimization]], [[invoicing-collections-and-dunning]] |
| **Account updater** | A card-network service that gives the provider a replaced card's new number or expiry date, so a stored card keeps working after the bank reissues it. | [[authorization-optimization]], [[invoicing-collections-and-dunning]] |
| **Routing / routing rule** | Routing is the choice of path for one payment; a routing rule is the stated condition that makes the choice, such as “payments for this studio go to this provider”. | [[payment-routing-and-orchestration]] |
| **Least-cost routing** | Sending each debit payment over whichever of the networks the card can travel on costs less in fees. | [[payment-routing-and-orchestration]], [[payment-economics]] |
| **Payment orchestration** | A layer in front of two or more providers that decides, per payment, which one processes it. Its parts are one payment API, a rule engine, an adapter per provider, a credential store and an attempt record. | [[payment-routing-and-orchestration]] |
| **Failover** | Sending payments to a second provider when the first is failing; the simplest use of orchestration, and worth little unless it has been rehearsed. | [[payment-routing-and-orchestration]], [[payment-reliability-and-availability]] |
| **Forwarding proxy** | A provider service to which you send a request addressed to a third party with placeholders for the card details; the provider fills them in from its vault and sends it on. | [[payment-routing-and-orchestration]], [[tokenization-vaulting-and-pci]] |
| **Card-present payment** | A payment where a terminal reads the physical card, or a phone standing in for it. | [[in-person-payments]] |
| **EMV** | The chip-card standard, named after Europay, Mastercard and Visa: the chip generates a one-time cryptogram for each transaction, whether the card is inserted or held near the reader (contactless). | [[in-person-payments]] |
| **EMV liability shift** | The network rule under which an issuer may dispute a counterfeit-card fraud on a chip card when the payment was not read at a chip-reading device or the full chip data was not passed on, so the loss falls on the merchant's side. It works through disputes and is not a law. | [[in-person-payments]] |
| **Payment terminal** | A certified computer with an expiry date that reads cards at a counter: a managed device with a fleet, software updates and keys. | [[in-person-payments]] |
| **Point-to-point encryption (P2PE)** | A solution validated against PCI's standard that protects card data from the point where the merchant accepts the card to the secure point of decryption. Both providers encrypt end to end (E2EE) by default and offer validated P2PE as an option. | [[in-person-payments]] |
| **Terminal API** | The interface through which point-of-sale software drives a terminal, either inside the store (local) or through the provider's platform (cloud). | [[in-person-payments]] |
| **Offline payment (offline EMV / store-and-forward)** | Two different things. In offline EMV the terminal checks the PIN against the chip and asks the card to approve. In store-and-forward the terminal approves without any verification, stores the payment and sends it for authorization when the connection returns: credit extended by the merchant. | [[in-person-payments]] |
| **Unified commerce** | Treating a store payment and an online payment as one. In data terms it is three separate checks: a shared identifier for the payer, a refund that crosses channels, and one settlement report covering both. | [[in-person-payments]], [[case-adyen]] |
| **Tap to pay on a phone** | Accepting contactless cards on an ordinary phone: it removes the terminal hardware and keeps the acquirer and the certification. | [[in-person-payments]] |

## <a id="g-ledger"></a>Part III — LEDGER: Recording and Moving Money

Part III's terms describe how a payment company records whose money it holds, what it owes a merchant at each moment, how that money leaves, how the records are proved against one another, and who is allowed to hold it. The contrasts that matter are *pending* versus *available*, *sent* versus *arrived*, and a *webhook* versus a *report*.

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ledger (double-entry ledger)** | An append-only record in which every movement of value is written as lines that balance, so that a missing movement shows up as an account that does not empty. The simplest form, a list of who owes whom, is as old as credit at the village shop. | [[payment-ledgers]], [[how-payments-got-complicated]] |
| **Account / entry** | An account is a named bucket of value with a balance, such as “owed to Studio Noord”. An entry is a set of signed lines, each against one account, that sum to zero. | [[payment-ledgers]] |
| **Immutable entry** | A ledger entry is never edited or deleted; a mistake is corrected by a further entry. | [[payment-ledgers]] |
| **Clearing account** | An account that holds an amount only while a process with several steps is in flight, and must return to zero when the process completes. | [[payment-ledgers]] |
| **Fund flow** | The fixed path an amount takes through accounts for one kind of business event: a state machine in which the accounts are the states and the entries the transitions. The term is Stripe's (2024). | [[payment-ledgers]] |
| **Balance (merchant balance)** | The net amount the provider's books say it owes the merchant at a given moment: sales credited, less everything debited, less what has already been paid out. The merchant holds a claim, not money. | [[balances-and-funds-flows]] |
| **Pending funds / available funds** | Pending funds are owed to the merchant and not yet payable; available funds are payable now and are what refunds are taken from. Adyen uses the two words differently (see the crosswalk). | [[balances-and-funds-flows]] |
| **Reserve** | Money owed to the merchant but held back by the provider against refunds and chargebacks that may still come. The word names a different thing at Adyen (see the crosswalk). | [[balances-and-funds-flows]] |
| **Negative balance** | A balance in which the debits exceed the credits, so the merchant owes the provider. | [[balances-and-funds-flows]], [[splits-balances-and-platform-payouts]] |
| **Pre-funding** | The provider paying the merchant before the network has paid the provider, and carrying that risk. Adyen's documentation describes it for sales-day payout (capped at 30 days, then reversed), while its annual report for 2025 calls paying only after funds are received the default setup; the two texts are not reconciled in public. Stripe documents an advance for Instant Payouts only. | [[balances-and-funds-flows]], [[clearing-and-settlement]] |
| **Payout** | The provider sending money it owes you, from a balance in its own books, to a bank account outside the provider. It is two processes that finish at different times: the accounting debit and the delivery by a bank network. | [[payouts-and-money-movement]] |
| **Payout rail** | The bank network that carries a payout to the receiving bank, such as SEPA Credit Transfer, SEPA Instant, ACH, Faster Payments or a wire. | [[payouts-and-money-movement]] |
| **Cut-off time / value date** | A rail's cut-off time is the point after which an instruction waits for the next processing cycle. The value date is the date from which the receiving bank counts the money as the payee's. | [[payouts-and-money-movement]] |
| **Payout return** | The receiving bank sending the money back after the rail accepted the transfer. It reaches the sender as a new transaction, days after the payout was marked sent. | [[payouts-and-money-movement]] |
| **Return window** | The time a sent payout is kept open before it is marked arrived by inference. No source gives an upper bound, so the window is a policy you choose, and a later return must still be handled. | [[payouts-and-money-movement]] |
| **Instant payout** | A payout over a rail that reports the credit within seconds or minutes, sold by both providers with a fee and a ceiling. | [[payouts-and-money-movement]] |
| **Verification of payee / Confirmation of Payee** | A check, before a transfer is sent, that the name given matches the name on the receiving account. Verification of payee is the EU's rule (applied in the euro area since 9 October 2025); Confirmation of Payee is the older UK service. | [[payouts-and-money-movement]] |
| **IBAN** | International bank account number. Bank transfers are routed by account identifier and not by name, which is why a misdirected payout succeeds. | [[payouts-and-money-movement]] |
| **Reconciliation** | Showing that two independently written records of the same money agree, and explaining every difference. | [[payment-reconciliation-and-reporting]], [[how-payments-got-complicated]] |
| **Provider-side reconciliation** | The provider reconciling its own ledger against two outside records: the clearing file, in which a network or acquirer lists each transaction it processed and what it will pay, and the bank statement. | [[payment-reconciliation-and-reporting]] |
| **Exception / exception aging** | An exception is an item that does not match. Exception aging means recording how long each has been open and who owns it; age separates timing differences from missing money. | [[payment-reconciliation-and-reporting]] |
| **Settlement report** | The provider's itemized file of what it has settled and paid, with fees and adjustments, as of a stated cut-off. A report is complete and late; a webhook is prompt and incomplete. They are different contracts. | [[payment-reconciliation-and-reporting]] |
| **Event-fed / report-driven / warehouse-driven close** | Three ways to close a day: post every webhook to the books as it arrives; trust the provider's report as of its cut-off; or make the same match with queries on exported data. | [[payment-reconciliation-and-reporting]] |
| **Bank (credit institution)** | An undertaking that takes deposits and grants credits for its own account. Money left with a bank becomes the bank's own asset, which it may lend, and the customer holds a claim on the bank. | [[licences-and-safeguarding]] |
| **Payment institution** | A licensed non-bank that may move customers' money and may not treat it as its own. | [[licences-and-safeguarding]] |
| **Electronic money institution (e-money institution)** | A licensed non-bank that may, in addition, keep a customer's money as a stored balance (e-money) to spend or withdraw later; it must hold those funds apart from its own and may not lend them. | [[licences-and-safeguarding]] |
| **Money transmitter** | In the United States, a non-bank that holds or moves money for others, licensed state by state. | [[licences-and-safeguarding]] |
| **Partner bank** | A licensed bank that a non-bank relies on for what only a bank may do: card-network membership (then called a sponsor bank) or deposit accounts. | [[licences-and-safeguarding]], [[case-stripe]] |
| **For-benefit-of (FBO) account** | One bank account held for many customers, where the bank sees a single balance. | [[licences-and-safeguarding]] |
| **Safeguarding** | What the law makes a licensed non-bank do with money it may not use: keep it apart from its own funds, in a separate account at a bank or in secure, low-risk assets, protected from the firm's other creditors. | [[licences-and-safeguarding]] |

## <a id="g-interface"></a>Part IV — INTERFACE: The API as the Product

Part IV's terms describe the contracts an API gives the developer for each case one call hides: an identity and a state, a key that makes a request safe to repeat, a pinned version, events for outcomes that arrive later, and the limits within which each holds.

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Resource model / command API** | A resource model is the set of things an API gives an identifier and lets you read again. The alternative is an API of commands: calls that do something and return a result, leaving nothing to fetch. | [[payment-api-design]] |
| **Stable identifier / normalized vocabulary** | Two of the three things integrator code should depend on: an identifier for each payment that does not change with the method, and one vocabulary of states and refusal reasons into which each new answer is mapped. The third is an explicit line between what the response answers and what arrives later. | [[payment-api-design]] |
| **Expandable object** | A related object that the API returns as an identifier by default and as the full object on request. | [[payment-api-design]] |
| **Metadata** | Key-value pairs the provider stores and returns without interpreting; the usual place for your own identifier. | [[payment-api-design]] |
| **Error model (no answer / rejected / refused)** | How an API says what went wrong. For payments it must keep three outcomes apart: no answer (a timeout; unknown whether anything happened), rejected (the provider turned the request away; nothing happened) and refused (the issuer said no). | [[payment-api-design]] |
| **API family** | A group of endpoints with its own version counter and conventions, and usually its own host, built for one kind of job. | [[payment-api-design]] |
| **Idempotent** | Said of an operation that can be called any number of times while its side effects occur only once. | [[idempotency-and-retries]] |
| **Idempotency key** | An identifier sent with a request so that the provider can recognize a repeat of the same request and not perform it twice. It makes the request repeatable; it does not make the outcome known. | [[idempotency-and-retries]] |
| **Request fingerprint** | The server's stored record of what the first request asked for (its parameters, or a hash of them), used to tell a true repeat from a different request sent under the same key. | [[idempotency-and-retries]] |
| **Recovery point** | In a stored idempotency record, the name of the last step that completed, so that a retried request resumes there. | [[idempotency-and-retries]] |
| **Unknown outcome** | The state in which the client cannot tell whether money moved, typically after a timeout. A request has three outcomes, not two: approved, declined and unknown. | [[idempotency-and-retries]], [[payment-authorization]] |
| **Business event (as the source of a key)** | The thing a customer or a schedule meant to happen once: a cart, an invoice, a renewal attempt, a refund request, a payout period. The key is derived from it and stored before the first send; a deliberate new attempt is a new business event. | [[idempotency-and-retries]] |
| **Retry policy** | The rule for which failures are retried, after what wait and how many times. For failed requests it means backoff with jitter under the same key; for refused payments it says which refusals are retried and when, within the networks' limits. | [[idempotency-and-retries]], [[authorization-optimization]] |
| **API version** | The shape of the API one integration sees. The provider remembers it on your behalf for years, so that an integration written long ago keeps working while the API changes around it. | [[payment-api-versioning]] |
| **Breaking change** | A change to an API after which client code that was correct can fail or misbehave without having been modified. | [[payment-api-versioning]] |
| **Version pinning** | Fixing the version of the API that one integration sees, so that the provider's changes reach it only when it asks for a newer version. A pin works like a loan: it buys time, and the amount owed grows with every release. | [[payment-api-versioning]] |
| **Version-change module** | Stripe's published mechanism (2017) for serving old versions: each incompatible change is encapsulated in a module that documents it and transforms the response for callers on an older version. | [[payment-api-versioning]] |
| **Deprecation policy** | A provider's published lifecycle that ends old versions after a stated notice period. | [[payment-api-versioning]] |
| **Event** | The provider's record that the state of something changed. | [[payment-webhooks-and-events]] |
| **Webhook** | The delivery of an event as an HTTP request to an endpoint you registered. It is a notice to store and then confirm against the provider's own record, not the record itself. | [[payment-webhooks-and-events]] |
| **At-least-once delivery** | Every event is delivered one or more times; no sender can promise exactly once across a network. Its consequences are the duplicate (an event that comes a second time) and disorder (an older event arriving after a newer one). | [[payment-webhooks-and-events]] |
| **Retry window** | The period after which an unacknowledged event is not sent again. | [[payment-webhooks-and-events]] |
| **Signature verification** | Recomputing a keyed hash (an HMAC, hash-based message authentication code) over the received message with a secret only you and the provider hold, to prove who sent it and that nobody changed the signed part. | [[payment-webhooks-and-events]] |
| **Snapshot event / thin event** | A snapshot event carries a copy of the object as it was when the event was created, in the shape of one API version; a thin event carries only the type and identifiers, so the receiver has to fetch the current object. | [[payment-webhooks-and-events]] |
| **Sweep (pending-payment job)** | A job that takes every payment pending longer than a set time and resolves it from the provider's record; needed for the events that never arrive. (Adyen uses “sweep” for a scheduled payout from a balance account; see the crosswalk.) | [[payment-webhooks-and-events]] |
| **Least privilege** | Each calling system holds a credential that can do its own task and nothing else. | [[payment-developer-platform]] |
| **Test environment** | A copy of the API in which calls create simulated objects and no card network or bank is contacted; a test card is a card number that works only there and returns a known result. | [[payment-developer-platform]] |
| **Rate limit** | A cap on what one caller may send; a caller who exceeds it gets HTTP 429. A refusal for this reason is not a failed payment. | [[payment-developer-platform]], [[payment-peak-load]] |
| **Load shedding** | Dropping lower-priority requests from everyone when the system itself is overloaded, so that critical ones keep working; the decision rests on the state of the whole system, not on who is calling. | [[payment-developer-platform]] |
| **Token bucket** | A counter refilled at a fixed rate: each request takes a token and waits when none is left. Used by providers to limit and by clients to pace a batch below a limit. | [[payment-developer-platform]], [[payment-peak-load]] |

## <a id="g-trust"></a>Part V — TRUST: Card Data, Fraud, Authentication and Compliance

Part V's terms cover five controls, each defined by a rule that assigns responsibility: who may see a card number, what stands in for it, how fraud is estimated, how the payer is authenticated and what a provider must know about its customers. Legal entries summarize the rule as of November 2026; the cited source wins.

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **PCI DSS** | The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard: the card industry's security standard for anyone who stores, processes or transmits card data. | [[tokenization-vaulting-and-pci]] |
| **Primary account number (PAN)** | The card number, the main thing PCI DSS protects. | [[tokenization-vaulting-and-pci]] |
| **PCI DSS scope / cardholder data environment** | Scope is the set of systems the standard reaches. Its core, the cardholder data environment, is every server, queue, log and support tool that stores, processes or transmits a card number, plus anything that can affect their security. Scope is decided by architecture. | [[tokenization-vaulting-and-pci]] |
| **Self-assessment questionnaire (SAQ)** | The form on which a business attests that it meets PCI DSS. Which one applies follows from the integration: SAQ A when every card input is on a page or in a frame the provider serves, SAQ A-EP when your own page hosts the form, SAQ D when your systems are on the path. | [[tokenization-vaulting-and-pci]] |
| **Hosted field** | A card input inside an iframe that the provider serves from its own domain. Your page decides where it sits and how it looks, and the browser's same-origin policy stops your scripts from reading what is typed. | [[tokenization-vaulting-and-pci]] |
| **Script criterion** | The line of SAQ A that asks you to confirm your page cannot be used for a script attack; it applies to a page that embeds the provider's payment form and not to one that redirects. | [[tokenization-vaulting-and-pci]] |
| **Token / provider token** | A token is a reference that stands for a card number. A provider token is an identifier for a stored card that only this provider can turn back into a card number, so it works only there. | [[tokenization-vaulting-and-pci]], [[how-payments-got-complicated]] |
| **Vault** | The provider's store of real card numbers, kept apart from its own other systems so that most of the provider cannot see a card number either. | [[tokenization-vaulting-and-pci]] |
| **Network token** | A substitute card number that the card network issues for one card at one merchant or device and maps back to the real card during authorization. It stays valid when the card is reissued. | [[network-tokens]] |
| **Token requestor** | The party that asks the network for a network token and is the only party that may use it; in practice the provider or the wallet. | [[network-tokens]] |
| **Token service provider** | The party that issues a network token and holds the mapping to the card; in practice the card network's token service. | [[network-tokens]] |
| **Provisioning** | The request that creates a network token: the requestor sends the card number to the network, the issuer approves and the network issues the token. The issuer can say no. | [[network-tokens]] |
| **Cryptogram** | A one-time value generated for one use of one token (or, on a chip card, for one transaction), proving that the request comes from the token's legitimate holder. | [[network-tokens]], [[in-person-payments]] |
| **Domain control** | The restriction of a network token to the party, channel or device it was requested for: a token stolen from one merchant or provider is useless at another, which is also why a token belongs to its requestor. | [[network-tokens]] |
| **Device token** | A network token whose requestor is a phone wallet and whose restriction is the device; the cryptogram is produced when the owner unlocks the phone. | [[network-tokens]], [[journey-card-payment]] |
| **Credential lifecycle** | What happens to a stored card over time: when the bank reissues the card, the network re-points the network token to the new card, so the stored credential keeps working without the customer doing anything. | [[network-tokens]] |
| **Card testing** | Trying stolen card numbers on a small payment at one merchant to learn which still work before using them elsewhere. The tested merchant pays the fees. | [[payment-fraud-and-risk]] |
| **Account takeover / merchant fraud** | Account takeover: a criminal logs in as a real customer and pays with the stored card. Merchant fraud: a fraudulent business takes payments without providing the goods or services, and the loss falls on the platform or provider if the seller's funds are gone. | [[payment-fraud-and-risk]] |
| **Risk score** | The provider's estimate of how likely a payment is to end in a fraud dispute. A provider does not detect fraud during an authorization; it estimates and blocks above a threshold. | [[payment-fraud-and-risk]] |
| **Fraud signal** | An input to the risk estimate; most of its value comes from the provider seeing many merchants. | [[payment-fraud-and-risk]] |
| **False positive** | A legitimate payment refused as fraud; because fraud is rare, the commonest error. The threshold is a price with two costs, fraud accepted and good customers refused. | [[payment-fraud-and-risk]] |
| **Label / holdout / feedback loop** | A label is the after-the-fact record that a payment was fraud, usually a dispute that arrives weeks later. A holdout is a small share of payments let through whatever their score so their outcomes can be seen. Without it the system's own decisions determine which outcomes it will later see: the feedback loop. | [[payment-fraud-and-risk]] |
| **Credit exposure** | What a provider or platform stands to lose on an account if its refunds and chargebacks exceed the funds it can still reach. | [[payment-fraud-and-risk]], [[splits-balances-and-platform-payouts]] |
| **Strong customer authentication (SCA)** | The EU's requirement, from PSD2, that the payer be authenticated with two or more independent elements of knowledge, possession and inherence. A merchant-initiated payment is outside the rule, because the payer is not initiating. | [[three-d-secure-and-sca]] |
| **PSD2** | The EU's second Payment Services Directive, (EU) 2015/2366: the current payment services law, source of strong customer authentication, the payment institution license and safeguarding. | [[three-d-secure-and-sca]], [[licences-and-safeguarding]] |
| **PSD3 / Payment Services Regulation (PSR)** | The EU directive and regulation that will replace PSD2. Not adopted on 17 November 2026: “Awaiting Parliament 2nd reading”. | [[payment-compliance-and-regulation]], [[payments-what-is-moving]] |
| **Exemption** | A case, listed in the technical standards, in which a provider may skip the two factors. The low-risk exemption (transaction risk analysis) is rationed by the provider's own fraud rate. | [[three-d-secure-and-sca]] |
| **3-D Secure** | The card networks' protocol for letting the issuer authenticate the payer of an online payment. Three parties each run one component: a 3DS server on the merchant's side, a directory server (DS) at the network and an access control server (ACS) at the issuer. | [[three-d-secure-and-sca]] |
| **Frictionless flow / challenge flow** | In a frictionless flow the issuer authenticates in the background from the data it is sent, with no further action by the customer; in a challenge flow it asks the customer to act, for example in a banking app. | [[three-d-secure-and-sca]] |
| **Liability shift** | The card networks' rule that a payment authenticated through 3-D Secure, if later disputed as fraud, is the issuer's loss and not the merchant's. It covers fraud disputes only. | [[three-d-secure-and-sca]] |
| **Data-only flow** | Using the 3-D Secure channel to share data with the network while requesting no authentication. | [[three-d-secure-and-sca]] |
| **Passkey** | A credential held on the customer's device and unlocked by a fingerprint or face scan; the card networks' versions are in pilot. | [[three-d-secure-and-sca]], [[payments-what-is-moving]] |
| **KYC / KYB** | Know your customer and, for a business customer, know your business: the duty of a firm that moves money (an obliged entity under anti-money-laundering law) to know who its customers are. | [[payment-compliance-and-regulation]] |
| **Beneficial owner** | Any natural person who ultimately owns or controls a legal entity; the EU's AML Regulation sets the ownership test at 25% or more. | [[payment-compliance-and-regulation]] |
| **Sanctions screening / PEP screening** | Sanctions screening compares the business and its owners with government lists of persons nobody may deal with. A politically exposed person (PEP) is not forbidden as a customer but gets closer checks. | [[payment-compliance-and-regulation]] |
| **Transaction monitoring** | Watching transactions throughout a business relationship to check that they are consistent with what the firm knows of the customer; a suspicion is reported, confidentially, to the country's financial intelligence unit. | [[payment-compliance-and-regulation]] |
| **Prohibited and restricted businesses** | A provider's own list, on top of the law, of businesses it will not serve or serves only with approval; a lawful business can still be refused. | [[payment-compliance-and-regulation]] |
| **Capability / requirement / deadline** | The three things in which both providers hold the result of verification. A capability is a named permission on an account, such as taking card payments or receiving payouts; a requirement is a missing fact or document; a deadline is the date by which it must be met, after which the capability is switched off. | [[platform-onboarding-and-verification]], [[payment-compliance-and-regulation]] |
| **The form / the pass-through / the mirror** | Three ways a platform can hold verification. The form stores nothing after sign-up; the pass-through stores a yes or no per seller and leaves the rest to the provider's hosted pages and notices; the mirror stores each seller's requirements, deadlines and capability status, with an owner. | [[payment-compliance-and-regulation]], [[platform-onboarding-and-verification]] |
| **Re-verification** | A provider asking again, months or years after onboarding, for confirmation that what it knows about a business is still true. Verification is a standing state on an account, not a decision per payment. | [[payment-compliance-and-regulation]] |
| **DORA** | The Digital Operational Resilience Act, Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, in application since 17 January 2025: incident reporting and a register of technology suppliers become legal duties. It imposes no data-localization obligation. | [[payment-compliance-and-regulation]], [[payment-multi-region-and-data-residency]] |
| **EU AML Regulation** | Regulation (EU) 2024/1624, the directly applicable anti-money-laundering rulebook that applies from 10 July 2027; until then national laws under the earlier directive apply. | [[payment-compliance-and-regulation]] |

## <a id="g-revenue"></a>Part VI — REVENUE: Billing, Tax and Recognition

Part VI's terms belong to the second state machine above the payment: what is owed, for which period, when to ask, and what happens when the first attempt fails. Read *invoice* beside *payment*, and *billings* beside *cash* and *revenue*.

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Billing engine** | The system that decides what is owed, for which period and when to ask; the payment API only carries out the asking. It can be run by the payment provider, by a dedicated billing system or by your own code. | [[subscriptions-and-billing-engines]] |
| **Subscription** | A standing agreement to charge a customer on a schedule. | [[subscriptions-and-billing-engines]] |
| **Billing cycle / anchor** | The billing cycle is the repeating period a subscription bills for; its anchor is the point in time that fixes where each period starts. | [[subscriptions-and-billing-engines]] |
| **Proration** | Charging or crediting a fraction of a price because the customer changed plan part-way through a period. | [[subscriptions-and-billing-engines]] |
| **Billing scheduler** | The part of a billing engine that decides when: it finds subscriptions whose period has ended and triggers the next invoice. Half of a subscription's transitions come from the clock, with no request behind them. | [[subscriptions-and-billing-engines]] |
| **Period record** | Under Keel, the row Larkfield's booking app writes before the first attempt of a renewal: membership, period, amount due and a state of `open`, `paid` or `void`. It is the record of the debt where the provider holds no invoice. | [[subscriptions-and-billing-engines]], [[journey-failed-renewal]] |
| **Usage event** | One record that a customer consumed something at a given time. Duplicates are removed by an identifier the producer of the event assigns. | [[usage-metering-and-rating]] |
| **Meter** | The rule that turns many usage events into one quantity for a billing period, by sum, by count or by last value. | [[usage-metering-and-rating]] |
| **Rating** | The step that applies a price plan to a metered quantity. Invoice-time rating needs a count that is correct eventually; a real-time balance check puts a read of a nearly current total into the product's request path. | [[usage-metering-and-rating]] |
| **Overage** | Usage above the amount a plan includes, billed per unit. | [[usage-metering-and-rating]] |
| **Credit balance / commitment** | A credit balance is usage paid for or granted in advance and drawn down as usage is rated; a commitment is a promise of minimum spend over a period. | [[usage-metering-and-rating]] |
| **Invoice** | A numbered, dated statement of what one customer owes one seller, for which goods or which period: the record of a debt. A payment is something else, one attempt to move money; a failed payment leaves an open invoice open. | [[invoicing-collections-and-dunning]], [[subscriptions-and-billing-engines]] |
| **Finalized invoice** | An invoice after the step that makes it a fixed document that may no longer change; corrections are made by a credit note. | [[invoicing-collections-and-dunning]], [[usage-metering-and-rating]] |
| **Credit note** | A document that reduces what is owed on an invoice and leaves the original as it was. | [[invoicing-collections-and-dunning]] |
| **Void / uncollectible** | Void says the amount is not owed: the invoice was issued in error and is canceled, though its number stays on record. Uncollectible says the amount is owed and the seller has stopped expecting it. | [[invoicing-collections-and-dunning]] |

Part VII's terms describe one decision seen four times: when a platform stands between payer and seller, who is the merchant, who holds the money and who carries the loss. Most of the vocabulary here is provider-specific and is translated in the [platforms crosswalk](#g-x-platforms).

## <a id="g-scale"></a>Part VIII — SCALE: Reliability, Data and Operations

Part VIII's terms describe what is allowed to give way under failure, load and change so that the record of money does not. In a money path the order of priorities is the reverse of a content site's.

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Availability / availability target** | Availability is the share of time, or of requests, in which a service answers correctly (“five nines” is 99.999%). ## <a id="g-crosswalk"></a>The Stripe–Adyen Vocabulary Crosswalk: How to Read It

The definitions above are in the book's own words. Each provider's documentation uses its own, and **each vocabulary follows that provider's object model**. Stripe exposes a seller as one account; Adyen separates the legal entity, the account holder, the balance account and the transfer instrument. Adyen calls a capture, a cancel and a refund modifications, each a separate asynchronous request with its own reference; Stripe has no word for the group. Stripe speaks of disputes for cards and bank debits alike where Adyen speaks of chargebacks, unpaid direct debits included. The nine tables that follow set such differences side by side, one area at a time.

Read a row from the left. **Concept** is the book's neutral name. The next two columns give the word each provider's documentation uses, with field names and statuses in code type. **Note** says how far the translation holds: “Same function” where the two words can be swapped; “Divided differently” or “No one-to-one match” where one provider splits what the other keeps together; **false friend** where the same word means different things; and “No counterpart found in documentation, November 2026” where the owning chapter searched for a counterpart and found none. That last phrase is a dated search result and not proof of absence. The final column links to the chapter that treats the concept and cites the documentation behind both cells.

The pairing is this book's mapping and therefore an inference: **neither company publishes a translation to the other**. A row was included only if both provider terms appear with that meaning in a chapter of the book, or were confirmed on the provider's documentation page as fetched on 17 November 2026; the 6 rows of the second kind are marked “documentation” in the last column and their pages are listed under Sources and Evidence. Pairs that could not be confirmed either way were left out. A team that integrates both providers, as Larkfield does, should define its own neutral terms from these tables and map each provider's words onto them, instead of letting whichever provider it integrated first name its database columns and its states.
