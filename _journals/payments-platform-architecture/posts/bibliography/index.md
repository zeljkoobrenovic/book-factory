> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Find the public source behind any claim in the book, with its date, its evidence type and every page that relies on it, and learn how much each kind of source supports.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **Every public source is listed once, because a page cited it.** Each entry was harvested from a page's **Sources and Evidence** section and carries a stable R-number, its date or 'n.d.', its evidence type and the pages that cite it. An R-number identifies a source; it does not rank it, and nothing is added as further reading.
> * **The type and the date decide how much a source supports.** A regulation or a network rule supports a requirement, audited accounts support a reported figure, an annual letter supports what a private company chose to say, undated documentation supports an interface as read in November 2026, and an engineering post supports only what the company said it had built in the year of the post.
> * **Look the entry up before you rely on a statement about Stripe or Adyen.** Where the entry is an engineering post older than about three years or an undated page, check the current documentation first. No source describes Larkfield, which is fictional; the labeled inferences, recommendations and assumptions are mine.

<br>
Use this register to trace a claim in any chapter to its public source and to see when that evidence was published or read. The entries come from the chapters' own **Sources and Evidence** sections, which sort each chapter's sources into three groups (company, product and investor sources; standards, regulation and official documentation; research and practitioner sources) and mark old or second-hand evidence with **(evidence age: N years)** and **(secondary source)**. Research ran up to **17 November 2026**. **'n.d.' means undated**, so for those entries the access month is the only indication of when the page was read.

The register does what a chapter's own list cannot: it shows how many pages lean on one source and how old the evidence under a subject is. That matters here because the public record is uneven. Stripe's best-known posts on idempotency, rate limiting and versioning are from 2017, Adyen's account of its accounting design is from 2021, most documentation pages are undated, and Stripe's company figures come from annual letters while Adyen's come from audited reports.

The page first explains how to read an entry (R-numbers, dates, the age and secondary marks, the evidence types and what each supports, the counts by publisher, and what is left out), then gives a topic index, the entries grouped by publisher and, near the end, the sources by chapter.

## How to Read This Register

**R-numbers identify sources; they do not rank them.** The register contains **867 sources** harvested from the Sources and Evidence sections of **53 pages**. A document that chapters cite under two addresses (a documentation page and its Markdown export, a page with and without a country prefix) is one entry, with the other address kept as **Also cited at** where it leads somewhere different.

Identifiers stay stable. A new source receives the next unused number and a removed source retires its number, so an R-number written down today identifies the same document after the register is regenerated.

Each entry gives, in this order: the R-number, the linked title, the publisher or author, the date, the claim the first citing chapter uses it for, the evidence type in italics, and the pages that cite it. The publisher and date follow the chapters' citations; where chapters cited one document differently, the page itself was read in November 2026 and the chapters were corrected to agree.

**The date is the publication, version or last-updated date the source shows.** **'n.d.'** means the page shows no date: 581 of the 867 entries are undated, almost all of them documentation, and for those the access month (November 2026) is the only indication of when the text was read.

**The age mark flags old evidence.** An entry dated before November 2023 carries **(evidence age: N years)**, counted to the research cut-off of 17 November 2026, as the chapters mark it in their own source lists. 90 entries carry the mark. For a legal text the mark is a reminder to check that it is still in force; for an engineering post it means the system described may have been replaced, and the citing chapter says whether current documentation confirms the old account.

**The secondary mark flags evidence that is not first-party.** 23 entries carry **(secondary source)**: trade press, a law firm's or an analyst's briefing, or one company writing about another's rules. A chapter uses such a source only to fill a gap and names it as secondary in the sentence that cites it.

**The evidence type says how much the source can support.** The table gives each type, the number of entries of that type, what it contains and what it supports.

Two things follow from the table. Most of what the book says about either provider's interface rests on undated documentation read in one month, so it describes November 2026 and nothing later. And the engineering evidence reaches back years: the oldest Stripe engineering source cited is R426 (2014) and the oldest Adyen one is R756 (2018). Stripe's company figures come from annual letters and newsroom releases, Adyen's from an annual report with audited statements, so a figure from one is not set against a figure from the other as if they measured the same thing.

**What the register leaves out.** Private research notes and the book plan are excluded; their claims point to public sources, which are listed here. Cross-references to the companion books RECONCILED, ORDERED and LISTED are reading links, not evidence, and are excluded as well. The author's Productscape planning model appears once, as the last entry, as planning material that supports no claim. A statement the book marks **Not public** has no entry by definition: it records that the public record, searched up to the research cut-off of 17 November 2026, does not say. ## Topic Index

Entries are grouped by who published the source, because in this book the publisher and the kind of document decide how much a source supports. Each group below gives its range of identifiers, its number of entries and its most widely cited source; a range runs from the group's lowest to its highest identifier, and because a new source takes the next unused number, ranges can overlap. The most widely cited source in the whole register is R1, *Visa Core Rules and Visa Product and Service Rules*, cited in 15 pages. To start from a chapter instead, use **Sources by Chapter** near the end.

**Card networks, payment schemes and their rules (R1–R17, 17 sources).** What the card networks and the payment schemes publish themselves: Visa's public rules and fee tables, the networks' annual filings and press releases, and the pages of scheme operators such as iDEAL, Pay.UK, the European Payments Initiative and The Clearing House. Mastercard's rule documents could not be retrieved in November 2026, so its rules are cited through Stripe's and Adyen's documentation. The most widely cited is R1, *Visa Core Rules and Visa Product and Service Rules*, cited in 15 pages.

**Regulators, law and standards (R18–R79, 62 sources).** Legal texts of the EU, its member states, the UK, the US and India, publications of central banks, supervisors and tax authorities, and the standards a payment system is built against: PCI DSS, EMV and IFRS 15. The most widely cited is R20, *Directive (EU) 2015/2366 (PSD2)*, cited in 6 pages.

**Stripe documentation (R80–R362, 283 sources).** Stripe's documentation, API reference, changelog, support site and status page. These pages are undated and change without notice; each was read in November 2026. The most widely cited is R80, *Idempotent requests*, cited in 7 pages.

**Stripe engineering, blog and newsroom (R363–R465, 103 sources).** Stripe's engineering and developer blogs, incident reports, Stripe Sessions talks, newsroom releases, guides and product pages. The most widely cited is R364, *Scaling your API with rate limiters*, cited in 5 pages.

**Stripe letters, terms and legal (R466–R492, 27 sources).** Stripe's annual letters, its services agreement and the legal, licence and pricing pages of its entities. Stripe is a private company: its company figures come from these letters and from its newsroom, not from audited accounts. The most widely cited is R467, *Acquirer Disclosure & Acquirer Terms*, cited in 8 pages.

**Adyen documentation (R493–R697, 205 sources).** Adyen's documentation, API reference and specifications, release notes, help center and status page. These pages are undated and change without notice; each was read in November 2026. The most widely cited is R493, *API idempotency*, cited in 8 pages.

**Adyen engineering, knowledge hub and newsroom (R698–R764, 67 sources).** Adyen's engineering posts and explainers in its knowledge hub, product pages and press releases about products and acquisitions. The most widely cited is R698, *Adyen closes Talon.One and Orb acquisitions*, cited in 10 pages.

**Adyen investor, terms and legal (R765–R790, 26 sources).** Adyen's annual report, half-year shareholder letters and business updates with their results releases, and its terms, licence and pricing pages. Adyen is listed: its annual report carries audited financial statements. The most widely cited is R765, *Annual Report 2025*, cited in 13 pages.

**Other companies (R791–R848, 58 sources).** Documentation, engineering posts, filings and announcements of companies other than Stripe and Adyen: PayPal, Checkout.com, Block, Orb, Uber, Airbnb, Apple, Google and others, most of them cited in the contrast cases and the billing chapters. The most widely cited is R819, *Orb documentation: Adyen integration*, cited in 4 pages.

**Research, talks and practitioner sources (R849–R866, 18 sources).** Trade press, law-firm briefings, analysts and independent engineers. These are secondary sources: the chapters use them to fill a gap and say so in the sentence that cites them. The most widely cited is R851, *Georgia approves Stripe application to form MALPB*, cited in 5 pages.

**The author's planning model (R867, 1 source).** The Productscape domain model the book was planned from. It is listed once so that the planning input is visible; it is not public and supports no claim.
