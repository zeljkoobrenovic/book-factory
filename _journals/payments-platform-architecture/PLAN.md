# SETTLED — Book Plan and Chapter Contracts

**SETTLED: Architecting Payments and Revenue Infrastructure**

*How payment and revenue infrastructure is actually built: how a payment is authorized, cleared, settled, recorded, paid out and disputed; how the APIs, ledgers, vaults, risk systems and billing engines behind it work; and where Stripe and Adyen made different architectural bets, and why.*

**Status:** first edition in progress · **Created:** 2026-11-17 · **Research cut-off:** 17 November 2026

## Positioning

The reader is an **engineer or architect who is new to payments**: someone who builds software for a living, has just been asked to integrate, operate, extend or evaluate payment and revenue infrastructure, and needs to understand how it is built before making or judging decisions about it. The reader is assumed to be software-literate (APIs, databases, queues, HTTP, basic distributed-systems vocabulary such as replication or retries need no explanation) but not to know payments vocabulary (issuer, acquirer, interchange, clearing, settlement, chargeback, 3-D Secure, network token, payment facilitator, safeguarding, dunning, merchant of record). Every such term is explained in plain language the first time it appears in each chapter.

The book takes the perspective of an **architect who builds on and around payment providers** and wants to know what is inside them, and speaks in the first person when giving judgement. Its two anchors are **Stripe and Adyen**: nearly every chapter says what each has publicly documented about the topic, what can only be inferred, and where the two differ. Other companies (PayPal/Braintree, Checkout.com, Block, Uber, Shopify, Paddle, Spreedly and others) appear only where a contrast clarifies an architectural choice.

The central idea: **a payment is a promise that takes days to keep, kept by systems that cannot see each other.** A customer taps "pay" and gets an answer in two seconds, but that answer is only the first of several: the authorization is a promise by the customer's bank, the capture is a claim, clearing and settlement move the money days later through networks the merchant never sees, the payout moves it again, and a dispute can take it back months afterwards. The provider's architecture exists to make that slow, multi-party, partly reversible process look like one API call, without ever losing or duplicating money. Every hard problem in the book (idempotency, ledgers, reconciliation, webhooks, tokens, risk, platform liability, reliability) comes from the gap between the instant answer and the slow truth. A payment is done when it is **settled** and every record of it agrees, not when the API returns 200.

The book is **evidence-led**: company-specific statements are documented facts with a source and a publication date; everything else is labeled as industry practice, reference-architecture inference, recommendation, illustration (the fictional platform) or assumption. Evidence older than about three years is flagged as possibly describing a replaced system. Where the public record is thin, the chapter is short and says so. Every chapter ends by separating what is **established**, what is **context-dependent** and what is **the author's synthesis**.

## Running Example

**Larkfield** is fictional: a Rotterdam software platform for independent fitness and wellness studios. Studios pay Larkfield a subscription; their members pay the studios through Larkfield for memberships, class passes and drop-ins, online and at the front desk; Larkfield splits each payment, keeps a fee and pays the studio out. It runs on **both Stripe and Adyen**, which is what lets every chapter compare the two on the same payment. See `_research/scenario-ledger.md` for the canonical facts (figures, systems, cast, incidents, the recurring payment "Mila's class pass", the 2027 Keel program). Every chapter's example must agree with it; extend it only by appending to the ledger.

## Teaching discipline

Every chapter in Parts I–VIII answers, in named sections, the questions the book exists for: **what problem this solves and why it is hard** (the gap between the instant answer and the slow truth), **how the mechanism works**, **which designs are available**, **what trade-offs they introduce**, and **how Stripe and Adyen each do it**. The last of these is a required section titled **"Stripe and Adyen: Where They Differ"** with a table that separates, per aspect, what each company has documented (with the year), what is not public, and what the difference suggests (labeled as inference). Where the two do not differ in any way the public record shows, the section says so in a few lines. Every chapter ends with **"What to Take Away"**, split into *Established*, *Depends on context* and *The author's synthesis*.

The recurring payment is **Mila's class pass** (ledger): each chapter says, in one short orientation paragraph near the top, where in the payment's journey it sits.

**No padding.** A chapter whose topic has little public evidence is short (2,000–3,000 words is acceptable) and states plainly where the evidence stops. The contracts below mark the chapters where this is expected.
