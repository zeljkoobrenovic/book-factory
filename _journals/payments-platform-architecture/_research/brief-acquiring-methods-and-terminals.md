## PART 1: Acquiring and network connectivity (chapters 06 and 01)

### 1.1 What an acquirer is, and what it is licensed to do

The card networks define the acquirer by what it signs and what it submits. Visa's glossary: an Acquirer is "A Member that signs a Merchant or Payment Facilitator, provides a Cash Disbursement to a Cardholder, or loads funds to a Prepaid Card, and directly or indirectly enters a Transaction into Interchange" [VERIFIED: https://usa.visa.com/dam/VCOM/download/about-visa/visa-rules-public.pdf, published 2026-10-24]. Two things follow from the wording. An acquirer is a *member* of the network, so acquiring is a membership right, not a technical capability. And "directly or indirectly" means the acquirer need not operate the connection itself; it may use a processor [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

Stripe's services agreement defines the same role from the merchant's side, for any payment method and not only cards: a "Payment Method Acquirer" is "an entity that a Payment Method Provider has authorized to (a) sponsor or submit Transactions at the request of merchants to the Payment Method Provider for authorization and clearing; and (b) receive and remit settlement funds for authorized and cleared Transactions" [VERIFIED: https://stripe.com/en-us/legal/ssa-services-terms, n.d., accessed 2026-11; the general agreement is marked "Last modified: September 28, 2026"]. This is the most useful one-sentence definition I found for the book: the acquirer submits on the merchant's behalf and receives the settlement money.

Adyen's explanation for merchants adds the licensing point: "Acquirers must be licensed by local financial regulators and card schemes to relay transactions. They get this license through a long and complex administrative process, which involves compliance with financial institution regulatory requirements, as well as card scheme requirements" [VERIFIED: https://www.adyen.com/knowledge-hub/acquiring-bank, n.d., accessed 2026-11].

So there are two permissions, and they are separate [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]:

| Permission | Granted by | What it allows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A regulatory licence | The financial regulator of the country or region (bank, e-money institution, payment institution, state money transmitter, or a special-purpose charter) | To provide the regulated service of acquiring payment transactions and to hold or move the merchant's funds |
| Network membership or sponsorship | Each card network or local scheme | To submit transactions into that network under an identifier and to take part in its settlement |

The regulatory side shows in the legal texts of both anchor companies. Stripe Technology Europe Limited "is authorised as an electronic money institution by the Central Bank of Ireland (reference number: C187865) to issue electronic money, execute payment transactions, make money remittances, issue payment instruments and acquire payment transactions, and provide payment initiation services and account information services" [VERIFIED: https://stripe.com/stel/legal, published 2019-04-05] **(evidence age: 7 years; the page is still live and Stripe's current acquirer disclosure, below, confirms the entity's role)**. "Acquire payment transactions" is a named regulated payment service in EU law; an entity needs a licence that includes it [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT; the service is listed in Annex I of PSD2, not quoted here].

### 1.2 Membership tiers, BIN sponsorship and sponsor banks

Visa's public rules show how membership is tiered and how a non-principal reaches the network. A network identifier (a BIN for issuing, an Acquiring Identifier for acquiring) is licensed to a member, and the rules tie liability to the licence:

- "A Principal-Type Member must license and use only its assigned BIN or Acquiring Identifier." An "Associate-Type Member must either" license its own identifier, in which case one "licensed to an Associate-Type Member on or after 22 July 2017 must have only one Sponsor", or "Use a BIN or an Acquiring Identifier licensed to its sponsoring Principal-Type Member". A "Participant-Type Member does not have the right to license a BIN or an Acquiring Identifier, and must use only a BIN or an Acquiring Identifier designated to it by its Sponsor" [VERIFIED: Visa Core Rules, section 2.2.1.1, published 2026-10-24].
- Liability follows the identifier: "A Principal-Type Member is responsible and liable for all activities associated with any BIN or Acquiring Identifier that it licenses", and for an associate, "To the extent an Associate-Type Member is unable to fulfill that responsibility, its Sponsor will be responsible and/or liable" [VERIFIED: same section].
- A licensee "must not sell, rent, or exchange any BIN or Acquiring Identifier" [VERIFIED: Visa Core Rules, published 2026-10-24].

That is the mechanism behind the trade terms "sponsor bank" and "BIN sponsorship": a company that is not itself a principal member processes under an identifier that belongs to a member bank, and the bank answers to the network for what happens under it [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. The Georgia regulator's description of its special charter, as reported, uses exactly these words: with the charter "merchant acquirers can gain direct access to Visa and Mastercard rather than contract with a third-party bank sponsor to use the bank's identification number" [WIDELY REPORTED: https://www.pymnts.com/?p=4190428, 2026; trade press quoting the Georgia Department of Banking and Finance].

Mastercard uses "principal" and "affiliate" for the same two levels [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT; Mastercard Rules not fetched].

### 1.3 Processor, acquirer, gateway, payment facilitator

| Role | What it does | Member of the network? | Holds the merchant's money? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Acquirer | Signs the merchant (or a payment facilitator), submits transactions, receives settlement | Yes (principal, or associate under a sponsor) | Yes, between network settlement and payout |
| Acquirer processor | Runs the technical connection and message processing for an acquirer | No: Visa lists "Acquirer Processor" as "A non-Member agent or processor that a Member has engaged" | No |
| Gateway | Passes payment data from the merchant to an acquirer or processor; no settlement role | No | No |
| Payment facilitator (PayFac) | Contracts with an acquirer and signs many small "sponsored merchants" under it | No, but registered with the network by its acquirer | Usually receives settlement from the acquirer and pays the sponsored merchants |
| Payment service provider (PSP) | A commercial label for a company that packages some or all of the above | Depends on the PSP and the country | Depends |

[The Visa glossary line is VERIFIED: Visa Core Rules, published 2026-10-24. The rest of the table is ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT, industry usage.]

The payment-facilitator model, in Visa's public rules:

- Definition: "An entity that contracts with an Acquirer to deposit Transactions, receive settlement from, or contract with an Acquirer on behalf of a Sponsored Merchant" [VERIFIED: Visa Core Rules, glossary, entry last updated October 2025, edition 2026-10-24].
- The acquirer stays liable: "A Sponsored Merchant will be treated as a Merchant of its Payment Facilitator's Acquirer. An Acquirer that contracts with a Payment Facilitator is liable for all acts, omissions, and other adverse conditions caused by the Payment Facilitator and its Sponsored Merchants" [VERIFIED: Visa Core Rules, section 5.3.1.2, published 2026-10-24].
- Registration and identification: "An Acquirer that contracts with a Payment Facilitator must ensure registration of its Payment Facilitator with Visa" and must obtain "a unique Payment Facilitator identifier" and ensure the facilitator "assigns a unique identifier to each Sponsored Merchant", both carried in every applicable transaction [VERIFIED: Visa Core Rules, published 2026-10-24].
- The size limit: "An Acquirer that contracts with a Payment Facilitator must enter into a direct Merchant Agreement with a Sponsored Merchant that exceeds USD 1 million in annual Transaction volume", before processing for a new sponsored merchant, or "within 2 years" for an existing one. There are exceptions, for example where the facilitator "has held the relationship with the Sponsored Merchant for at least 2 years with the same Acquirer" and reports to the acquirer. The facilitator "may continue to provide payment services (including settlement)" to such a merchant [VERIFIED: Visa Core Rules, section 5.3.1.4, published 2026-10-24]. ⚠ Thresholds in network rules change; quote the edition.

Stripe's own guide for platforms describes what becoming a registered payment facilitator takes: find an acquiring bank ("Platforms must approach acquirers with a business plan in order to establish a partnership and get sponsored"), obtain Level 1 PCI DSS certification, "Register as a payfac with each card network in each country", and "Re-register as a payfac with card networks annually" [VERIFIED: https://stripe.com/en-us/guides/payfacs, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. The guide is Stripe's sales argument for using Connect instead, so its time and cost estimates should be cited as Stripe's, not as neutral figures [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

One company can hold several of these roles in different countries. Stripe's acquirer disclosure (section 1.6) shows Stripe as acquirer in some countries and as a payment facilitator of named banks in others; Block states in its annual report that "special operating rules that apply to us as a “payment facilitator”" and that "For a majority of our transactions, we do not directly access the payment card networks" [VERIFIED: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1512673/000162828026012254/xyz-20251231.htm, published 2026-02-26].

### 1.4 A new route in the United States: Georgia's merchant acquirer charter

In the US, direct membership of Visa and Mastercard has in practice required a bank, which is why non-bank processors work through sponsor banks [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. Georgia created a narrow state charter, the Merchant Acquirer Limited Purpose Bank (MALPB), for this one purpose.

- "Under Georgia law, MALPBs are limited in their activity ... MALPB charters are prohibited from engaging in general banking activities, may not solicit or accept deposits from the general public, and may not sponsor ATMs or issue payment cards." The charter "will allow the financial services company to obtain direct membership with Visa and Mastercard and to process payments without a sponsoring bank" [WIDELY REPORTED: https://www.icba.org/newsroom/news-and-articles/2025/07/08/georgia-approves-stripe-application-to-form-malpb, 2025; trade association news].
- Stripe's application "was accepted on March 31" 2025, with a 90-day decision period; a Stripe spokesperson called it "an extension of its work to expand the number of banking and other partners it works with" [WIDELY REPORTED: https://www.bankingdive.com/news/stripe-georgia-special-banking-charter-credit-card-payments/744921/, 2025; trade press quoting the regulator's spokesperson].
- Georgia approved it in early July 2025 (ICBA, above); trade press later described this as conditional approval and reported that "Stripe's MALPB received permission to begin business in May of this year" (2026), that Fiserv "became the first MALPB to process card transactions in April 2025", and that the charter dates from 2012 with Credorax Bank North America the first holder in 2014 [WIDELY REPORTED: https://www.pymnts.com/?p=4190428, 2026; and https://www.paymentsdive.com/news/checkoutcom-grabs-special-banking-charter/809404/, 2026; trade press].
- Checkout.com received conditional approval for the same charter in January 2026: "The MALPB charter is a pivotal step that allows us to operate as our own acquirer in the US" [WIDELY REPORTED: Payments Dive, 2026-01-12, as above].
- Primary confirmation that Stripe uses the charter: Stripe's acquirer disclosure lists, among the US acquirers of which "Stripe, LLC is a Payment Facilitator", "Stripe MALPB, 505 N Angier Avenue NE, Atlanta, GA, 30308" with its own "Stripe MALPB Acquirer Terms" [VERIFIED: https://stripe.com/en-us/legal/acquirer-disclosure, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. ⚠ What share of Stripe's US volume runs through it is not public.

**For readers new to payments.**
- *Acquirer*: the network member that submits a merchant's card transactions and receives the money for them from the network.
- *Sponsor bank*: a member bank that lets a non-member company process under the bank's network identifier and answers to the network for it.
- *Payment facilitator*: a company that signs many small merchants under one acquirer, pays them out, and is registered with the network by that acquirer.
- *Local acquiring*: processing a card payment through an acquirer in the cardholder's own country, so the network and the issuer treat it as domestic.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. (1) The acquirer of record is a per-country fact, so a provider's routing layer must choose an acquiring entity per transaction (by merchant country, card country, network and currency) and its ledger must keep funds per licensed entity; this is why "one API" hides several legal and money flows. (2) Where a provider is a payment facilitator, a bank sits between it and the network, which adds a settlement hop, a second set of rules (the bank's acquirer terms) and a dependency the provider cannot engineer away; a charter or licence removes the hop but adds capital, reporting and supervision. (3) For a platform such as the book's example, the practical question is not "who is the PSP" but "which entity acquires my sellers in each country, and what happens to a seller above the network's payment-facilitator threshold". (4) Local acquiring is a prerequisite for some domestic schemes, so method coverage (Part 2) and acquiring coverage are the same roadmap.

### 2.1 Method families, and the properties that actually differ

Both providers group methods into families, and the two taxonomies are close but not identical.

**Stripe's families**, as its overview lists them: Cards, Bank debits, Bank redirects, Bank transfers, Buy now pay later, Real-time payments, Stablecoin payments, Vouchers, Wallets, Gift cards. "Payment methods in the same family have similar features and checkout experiences. After you integrate one payment method, you can usually add another in the same family with minimal changes to your integration" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/payments/payment-methods/overview.md, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. One sentence per family is quotable: bank debits "pull funds directly from your customer's bank account ... Payment confirmation can take several business days"; with bank redirects "customers pay online by authenticating with their bank and approving the payment from their bank account"; with bank transfers "you give customers a virtual bank account number, and they push money to it"; with vouchers "customers complete online purchases in person"; with real-time payments "customers send money directly from their bank account or other funding source, using an intermediary such as a phone number or app to authenticate" [VERIFIED: same page].

**Adyen's catalog** classifies each method by a *payment type* and a *payment flow*. Counting the entries of the online-payments catalog as fetched on 17 November 2026 gives 113 entries: Wallets 32, Credit and debit cards 24, Online Banking 18, Buy Now Pay Later 17 (plus one listed as "Buy Now Pay Later, Direct Debit"), Prepaid and gift cards 5, Cash and ATM 5, Direct Debit 5, Real-time payments 4, Bank transfer 1. The flows are Direct, Redirect, "Additional action", await, qrCode and voucher [VERIFIED: https://docs.adyen.com/payment-methods.md, catalog content generated at fetch time, accessed 2026-11; the counts are my own tally of the page, and an entry is not always a distinct scheme: Klarna has four entries, Bancontact two]. Each entry lists its "Enabled features": Recurring, Refunds, Partial refunds, Multiple partial refunds, Separate captures, Partial captures, Multiple partial captures, Chargebacks, 3D Secure [VERIFIED: same page].

Those feature columns are, in effect, the list of things that differ between methods. Stripe documents the same dimensions per method as "payment method properties" or a capabilities block: payment confirmation timing (immediate or delayed), usability (single-use or reusable), manual capture, refunds with a submission window and processing time, disputes, and settlement timing [VERIFIED: e.g. https://docs.stripe.com/payments/bancontact.md and https://docs.stripe.com/payments/ach-direct-debit.md, n.d., accessed 2026-11].

The dimensions a chapter should teach [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT, synthesised from both sets of documentation]:

| Dimension | The question | The card answer | What other methods do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who starts the payment | Does the payer push money or does the merchant pull it? | Pull, against an authorization | Redirects, real-time rails and transfers are pushes; direct debits are pulls |
| Confirmation | When does the merchant know the outcome? | In seconds | Immediate for redirects, wallets and real-time rails; days for direct debits; days or never for vouchers and transfers |
| Authorization and capture | Is there a hold that can be captured later, partly, or several times? | Yes | Mostly no: the payment is one step ("direct capture"); BNPL and PayPal can separate |
| Finality | Can the payer take the money back? | Chargeback, months | None for iDEAL and Bancontact; unconditional for 8 weeks for SEPA Direct Debit; bank-dependent elsewhere |
| Refund | How does money go back, and until when? | Reversal on the same card | A new credit transfer, with a method-specific window, that can fail |
| Mandate | Is a standing permission needed, and who stores it? | Stored-credential agreement | Formal mandate for direct debits and for recurring on Pix and UPI |
| Settlement delay | When are funds available at the provider? | Days | From the same day to more than a week |

1. Apple Pay and Google Pay are card payments with a different credential; every card semantic (authorization, capture, chargeback) survives. PayPal and Klarna are their own networks with their own dispute rules, even though both APIs file them near wallets.
2. "No chargebacks" (iDEAL, Bancontact, Wero) is a scheme property, not a provider feature; "you cannot defend" (SEPA, Bacs) is the opposite scheme property. Neither provider can change either.
3. The two providers describe the *same scheme rule* in different vocabularies: Stripe calls a SEPA return a "dispute", Adyen a "chargeback", and Adyen also books an unpaid Bacs or SEPA debit (insufficient funds) as a chargeback, where Stripe reports a failed payment. An integrator who maps both to "dispute" will overcount fraud.

### 2.4 Direct debit: the right to take the money back

**The law (EU).** PSD2 gives the payer two different rights for a direct debit:

- An unconditional refund for eight weeks: "for direct debits as referred to in Article 1 of Regulation (EU) No 260/2012, the payer has an unconditional right to a refund within the time limits laid down in Article 77" (Article 76(1)), and "the payer can request the refund ... for a period of 8 weeks from the date on which the funds were debited"; the payer's provider must refund or justify a refusal "Within 10 business days" (Article 77) [VERIFIED: Directive (EU) 2015/2366, http://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32015L2366, published 2015-12-23] **(evidence age: 11 years; still in force; PSD3 and the Payment Services Regulation will replace it ⚠)**.
- Rectification of an unauthorised transaction: the user must notify "without undue delay ... and no later than 13 months after the debit date" (Article 71(1)) [VERIFIED: same text].

So "8 weeks / 13 months" is two rules: a no-questions refund of an *authorised* debit, and a claim that the debit was *never authorised* (no valid mandate). Both providers describe it that way (table in 2.2). Both put the eight-week sentence into the mandate text the payer accepts: Stripe's standard authorization text says "A refund must be claimed within 8 weeks starting from the date on which your account was debited" and Adyen's says "You must claim a refund within 8 weeks from the date your account was debited" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/payments/sepa-debit.md and https://docs.adyen.com/payment-methods/sepa-direct-debit.md, accessed 2026-11].

**Mandates in practice.** Stripe: "Checkout and the Payment Element automatically display the required mandate terms and record the customer's acceptance"; the mandate authorizes "your business and Stripe to send debit instructions". Adyen: "By default, Adyen uses its creditor ID on your behalf when collecting SEPA Direct Debit payments" (it prints the identifier, NL48ZZZ342764500000), the merchant must "Send a mandate confirmation to the shopper" containing the Unique Mandate Reference, must notify the customer "at least one day before initiating the SEPA Direct Debit payment", and "Adyen is only notified of a cancelled SEPA mandate when a payment is charged back" [VERIFIED: both pages above; the Adyen page was last modified 2026-11-17].

**UK.** The Direct Debit Guarantee: "If an error is made in the payment of your Direct Debit, by the organisation or your bank or building society, you are entitled to a full and immediate refund of the amount paid from your bank or building society"; changes must be notified "(normally 10 working days) in advance"; "Direct Debit is owned and managed by Pay.UK Limited" [VERIFIED: https://www.directdebit.co.uk/direct-debit-explained/direct-debit-guarantee/, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. The Guarantee has no time limit in its text, which is why a Bacs debit is never final in the card sense [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

**US.** The two providers state different consumer dispute windows for ACH: Stripe "up to 60 calendar days after a debit on a personal account, or up to two business days for a business account"; Adyen "up to two years after the settlement date" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/payments/ach-direct-debit.md and https://docs.adyen.com/payment-methods/ach-direct-debit.md, accessed 2026-11]. I could not verify either against Nacha's rules in this session. A plausible reconciliation is that 60 days is the network's return window for unauthorised consumer debits and two years the period in which a claim can still be raised outside the automated return process, but that is inference [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. Nacha's public pages do confirm the monitoring thresholds an originator is held to: an unauthorised-return rate threshold of "0.5 percent", and inquiry levels of "3.0 percent" for administrative returns and "15.0 percent" overall [VERIFIED: https://www.nacha.org/rules/ach-network-risk-and-enforcement-topics, n.d., accessed 2026-11].

### 2.5 Real-time account-to-account rails

- **SEPA Instant.** Regulation (EU) 2024/886 requires the payee's provider, "within 10 seconds of the time of receipt of the payment order", to "make the amount of the payment transaction available on the payee's payment account". Euro-area providers had to offer receiving "by 9 January 2025" and sending "by 9 October 2025"; non-euro-area providers by "9 January 2027" and "9 July 2027". Charges for instant transfers "shall not be higher than" for other credit transfers, and the payer's provider must offer "a service ensuring verification of the payee" before authorisation [VERIFIED: http://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32024R0886, published 2024-03-19].
- **FedNow.** "The FedNow Service went live on July 20, 2023. It is available to depository institutions in the United States"; it provides "interbank clearing and settlement that enables funds to be transferred ... in near real-time and at any time, any day of the year"; the first release includes "request for payment capability" as an optional feature [VERIFIED: https://www.federalreserve.gov/paymentsystems/fednow_about.htm, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. FedNow is a rail between banks, not a checkout method; neither Stripe's nor Adyen's method list fetched today names FedNow as a payment method [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT on the absence; searched the Stripe overview page and the Adyen catalog].
- **Pix.** "Pix is a real-time payment system developed by the Central Bank of Brazil" (Stripe); "built and owned by the Central Bank (Banco Central) and operated by Brazilian banks, digital accounts, and wallets", with the amount "transferred in real-time from the shopper's account to Adyen" (Adyen) [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/payments/pix.md and https://docs.adyen.com/payment-methods/pix.md, accessed 2026-11]. Central-bank primary pages could not be read.
- **UPI.** "UPI is a real-time payment system developed by the National Payments Corporation of India" (Stripe) [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/payments/upi.md, accessed 2026-11]. NPCI's own pages could not be read.

Real-time does not mean final for the merchant, and it does not mean same-day funds: Stripe settles Pix "Up to 2 business days (T+2)" and both providers document a 90-day dispute route for Pix (table in 2.2). Stripe states the general rule for every method: "A `succeeded` status indicates that the payment completed successfully according to the payment method's processing flow, but funds can still be refunded, disputed, returned, or reversed" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/payments/payment-methods.md, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. That sentence is the book's thesis in a provider's own words.

### 2.6 How Stripe abstracts payment methods

- **One object per instrument.** "The Payment Methods API allows you to accept a variety of payment methods through a single API. **For readers new to payments.**
- *Mandate*: the payer's standing permission for a named creditor to pull money from their account; the creditor or its provider must be able to produce it.
- *Finality*: the point after which the payer can no longer take a payment back through their bank; for some methods it is immediate, for others it never fully arrives.
- *Redirect flow*: the customer leaves the merchant's page to approve the payment in their bank's site or app, and may or may not come back.
- *Account-to-account payment*: money moves straight between two bank accounts over a bank rail, with no card network in between.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. (1) A payment record needs method-dependent state: "succeeded" for a card, an iDEAL payment and a SEPA debit are three different promises, so the consuming system should store the method family and derive what it may do (ship, grant access, refund, expect a reversal) from a per-family policy table, not from the status alone. (2) Fulfilment must be webhook-driven for every delayed or redirect method, because the browser may never return. (3) Refunds are outbound credit transfers for most non-card methods: they have their own state machine, can fail after appearing to succeed, and have method-specific windows (90 days for Pix, 180 for SEPA at Stripe, 730 for Bancontact and Wero). (4) A direct debit carries a liability tail of eight weeks unconditionally and thirteen months for mandate disputes, so balances, reserves and seller payouts on a platform need a method-aware risk horizon. (5) The mandate is data the merchant may need years later; whoever stores it (provider or merchant) must be explicit. (6) Scheme migrations such as iDEAL to Wero arrive as provider deadlines with dates set by the scheme; an integration that names methods in code has to change, one that delegates the list to the provider mostly does not.

## PART 3: In-person payments and unified commerce (chapter 11)

### 3.1 EMV chip and contactless: what the standard is and who owns the rules

- EMVCo's own description of its role: it exists "To facilitate the worldwide interoperability of secure payment transactions by developing and publishing the EMV Specifications and their related testing processes". It is not an enforcement body: "EMVCo does not mandate or enforce EMV compliance or the implementation policies for issuers, merchants, and acquirers, which are handled by payment networks independently outside of EMVCo" [VERIFIED: https://www.emvco.com/about-us/overview-of-emvco/, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- What the chip does: "EMV Chip technology uses advanced cryptography to generate a one-time security code (cryptogram) for each transaction that allows the card issuer and merchant point-of-sale terminal to authenticate the card. The security code is unique to each transaction and cannot be reused, which helps prevent counterfeit, lost and stolen fraud" [VERIFIED: https://www.emvco.com/emv-technologies/emv-contact-chip/, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Contact and contactless: contact chip payments "require physical contact with the acceptance terminal. Consumers insert or dip their chip card into a reader"; contactless supports "contactless chip cards and Near-Field Communication (NFC) enabled mobile devices", and "A contactless kernel provides a set of functions for the payment application in the acceptance device to process contactless transactions" [VERIFIED: https://www.emvco.com/emv-technologies/emv-contact-chip/ and https://www.emvco.com/emv-technologies/emv-contactless-chip/, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Certification: "EMVCo facilitates testing and approval of EMV Chip acceptance and payment devices ... EMVCo issues type approvals and security evaluation certificates" [VERIFIED: same contact-chip page]. A terminal therefore passes EMVCo type approval for its hardware and kernels, and then separate network and acquirer certification for the complete acceptance path; the second part is industry practice, not quoted [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].
- Deployment: "globally, 97% of card-based transactions are EMV Chip", in statistics "most recently reported as of Q4 2025" by American Express, Discover, JCB, Mastercard, UnionPay and Visa [VERIFIED: https://www.emvco.com/about-us/worldwide-emv-deployment-statistics/, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. The page's heading describes the measure as the share of *card-present* transactions that are EMV; the regional table is a download I did not fetch.

### 3.2 Card-present liability

The "liability shift" is a network dispute rule, not a law. Visa's public rules:

- It applies by region to "counterfeit POS and ATM Transactions" in the US region ("All domestic and interregional") and to "All domestic, intraregional, and interregional POS and ATM Transactions" in the Canada, CEMEA, Europe and LAC regions [VERIFIED: Visa Core Rules, Table 11-1, section 11.1.2, published 2026-10-24].
- An issuer may dispute under "Dispute Condition 10.1: EMV Liability Shift Counterfeit Fraud" when the transaction "was completed with a Counterfeit Card in a Card-Present Environment", "The Cardholder denies authorization", "The Card is a Chip Card", and one of: "The Transaction did not take place at a Chip-Reading Device", or it "was Chip-initiated and ... the Acquirer did not transmit the Full-Chip Data to Visa in the Authorization Request", or it "was approved offline, and the Acquirer did not transmit the Full-Chip Data to Visa in the Clearing Record" [VERIFIED: Visa Core Rules, Table 11-9, entry last updated October 2023, edition 2026-10-24].
- A second condition, 10.2, covers non-counterfeit (lost, stolen, not-received) fraud; "A Contactless Transaction" is listed among invalid disputes under it [VERIFIED: Visa Core Rules, Table 11-17, published 2026-10-24].

In plain words [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]: when a counterfeit copy of a chip card is used, the loss falls on whichever side did not support chip. If the merchant's terminal read the chip and the acquirer passed the full chip data on, the issuer keeps the loss; if the card was swiped at a non-chip device, the acquirer (and through its contract, the merchant) takes it. Two engineering consequences follow from the rule's wording: the liability protection depends on data the acquirer transmits, not only on the terminal, and an offline-approved transaction keeps it only if the full chip data reaches the clearing record.

### 3.3 The PCI standards for card-present: PTS, P2PE, MPoC

- **PTS** (PIN Transaction Security) is the device standard. PCI SSC lists "PTS Point of Interaction (POI)", "PIN Security" and "PTS Hardware Security Module (HSM)" among its standards [VERIFIED: https://www.pcisecuritystandards.org/standards/pts/, n.d., accessed 2026-11; only the navigation of this page was readable]. Both providers lean on it: "The payment terminals provided by Adyen are all PTS-approved Point-of-Interaction (POI) devices" [VERIFIED: https://docs.adyen.com/development-resources/pci-dss-compliance-guide.md, last modified 2024-02-27]; Adyen's terminal catalog labels each model "PCI PTS 5" or "PCI PTS 6" and states "PCI PTS 5 payment terminals will no longer be available for order starting April 30, 2026" [VERIFIED: https://docs.adyen.com/point-of-sale/what-we-support/select-your-terminals.md, last modified 2026-04-15]. PTS approvals expire by version, which is what forces hardware refresh cycles [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].
- **P2PE** (Point-to-Point Encryption): "A point-to-point encryption (P2PE) solution cryptographically protects account data from the point where a merchant accepts the payment card to the secure point of decryption ... Merchants using PCI-listed P2PE solutions also have fewer applicable PCI Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) requirements" [VERIFIED: https://www.pcisecuritystandards.org/standards/point-to-point-encryption-p2pe/, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- **MPoC** (Mobile Payments on COTS): it "builds on the existing PCI Software-based PIN Entry on COTS (SPoC) and PCI Contactless Payments on COTS (CPoC) Standards" and is "For entities developing, deploying, or managing solutions which accept both PIN and contactless cardholder data on the same COTS device" [VERIFIED: https://www.pcisecuritystandards.org/standards/mobile-payments-on-cots-mpoc/, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. MPoC and P2PE "are all separate PCI SSC standards intended for unique use cases" [VERIFIED: https://www.pcisecuritystandards.org/faqs/1457, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. COTS means commercial off-the-shelf: an ordinary phone or tablet.

### 3.5 Offline payments

"Offline" covers two different mechanisms, and Adyen's documentation separates them cleanly [VERIFIED: https://docs.adyen.com/point-of-sale/offline-payment.md, last modified 2026-05-04]:

- "**Offline EMV**: the payment terminal verifies that the PIN entered by the shopper matches the PIN on the EMV chip embedded in the card, and then asks the card to approve the payment. Whether the transaction is approved, depends on how the issuer configured the card. Because the card is in control, the payment can be processed in the clearing stream directly." "Most debit cards do not support this type of payment."
- "**Store and forward**: the payment terminal or Mobile SDK approves payments without any verification. Because of this, the risk of payments being declined by the issuer is higher ... In practice the authorization rates range from 95% in some industries to several percentage points higher."

The first is an approval by the card on the issuer's pre-set terms; the second is a merchant's decision to hand over goods and ask for authorization later [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

**For readers new to payments.**
- *EMV*: the chip-card standard; the chip produces a one-time cryptogram per transaction so that a copied card cannot be replayed.
- *Card-present*: a payment where the physical card or phone is read by a terminal, which carries different rules, fees and liability from an online payment.
- *P2PE*: encryption of card data from the reading device to the provider's decryption environment under a PCI-listed solution, which shrinks the merchant's compliance scope.
- *Store and forward*: accepting a payment while offline by storing it on the device and asking the issuer for authorization later, at the merchant's risk.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. (1) A terminal is a managed computer on someone else's network: fleet inventory, configuration, software versions, certificate and key rotation and PTS expiry dates are operational data the platform must hold per store. (2) The terminal API architecture is a resilience decision: cloud or server-driven is simplest and fails with the internet link; local or SDK-on-site survives it and costs network and certificate management. (3) Offline acceptance is credit extended by the merchant; limits (per transaction, per device, per period) belong in configuration with an owner, and stored transactions need their own reconciliation and retry path. (4) "Unified commerce" in data terms is three things: one shopper identifier across channels (card alias, payment account reference or generated card), one refund path across channels, and one settlement report; each should be tested separately, because a provider can deliver one without the others. (5) For a platform with thousands of small sites, putting all terminals on one provider is mostly a device-management and certification decision, not a pricing one.

## Open questions / not verifiable
